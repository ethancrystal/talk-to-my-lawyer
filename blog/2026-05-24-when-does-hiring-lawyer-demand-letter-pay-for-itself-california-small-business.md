# When Does Hiring a Lawyer for a Demand Letter Pay for Itself? A California Small Business Guide

Every small business eventually faces a customer or vendor who won't pay. Then the question becomes: is it worth paying a lawyer to chase a payment that may or may not come through?

**Short answer:** For most California small businesses, a flat-fee attorney demand letter starts paying for itself once the disputed amount hits roughly $500–$1,000. Below that, the cost can match the recovery. Above that, even a modest response rate makes the math work — and the upside is settlement without a court filing.

This guide walks through the actual numbers small business owners should run before deciding.

## How do small businesses calculate the break-even on a demand letter?

The break-even calculation has three variables:

1. **The flat fee for the letter.** Flat-fee attorney letters typically run between $99 and $500 depending on complexity. Talk to My Lawyer's first letter is free, which changes the math entirely — but for the rest of this guide we'll use a representative figure.
2. **The probability the letter prompts payment.** B2B response rates on a well-drafted attorney demand letter sit in a wide range — anywhere from 30% to 70% — depending on the size of the debt, whether the recipient is a business that values its credit and reputation, and whether there's any legitimate dispute. Estimates vary by industry and engagement.
3. **The amount you actually recover.** Sometimes that's the full balance. Sometimes it's a negotiated partial payment plus a release of claims.

The formula:

> **Expected recovery = (probability of payment × amount recovered if paid) − letter cost**

If that number is meaningfully positive, the letter is worth sending. If it's near zero, you're better off either writing it off or pursuing small claims yourself.

## When does the math work — and when doesn't it?

The math works most cleanly when:

- The amount owed is at least 3–5× the letter cost
- You have written documentation (invoice, contract, signed estimate)
- The debtor is a business or an individual with reachable assets
- The four-year statute of limitations on written contracts (California Code of Civil Procedure § 337) hasn't run

The math works less cleanly — or fails — when:

- The amount is so small that even full recovery barely covers the fee
- You have no written contract or invoice (you can still send a letter, but the leverage is weaker)
- The debtor has already declared bankruptcy or is judgment-proof
- The statute of limitations has expired

For most California small businesses chasing $1,500–$15,000, the break-even is comfortable. Even at a 40% recovery rate, the expected value beats the cost.

## What does an attorney-signed letter accomplish that a DIY version doesn't?

A self-written demand has the same legal weight as an attorney letter on paper. But in practice, three things change:

- The recipient knows a lawyer has reviewed the facts and considers the claim viable
- The recipient knows the next step is real — not another follow-up email
- The recipient's own attorney, if they have one, will often advise resolving quickly rather than running up defense costs

Attorneys also tend to draft the letter in a way that closes off common defenses: clear citation of the contract or invoice, statement of the legal basis (breach of contract, account stated, unjust enrichment), an explicit demand, and a specific deadline. That precision is often what moves a stalled negotiation.

## Real-world example: the math on a $2,400 dispute

Say you're a California Web design contractor. A client owes you $2,400 on a finished project, and after 60 days of polite follow-ups they've stopped replying.

Your options:

- **Write it off.** Net result: −$2,400.
- **Send your own follow-up email.** Best case: $2,400. Realistic case: the same silence you've already gotten. Estimated expected value: low.
- **Pay for an attorney demand letter at $199.** A reasonable estimated response rate for a documented B2B invoice with a signed contract is 40–60%. At the low end (40%), expected value = (0.40 × $2,400) − $199 = **$761 net positive**. At the high end (60%), expected value = (0.60 × $2,400) − $199 = **$1,241 net positive**.
- **File in small claims yourself.** Filing fee is $50–$75, you may wait 30–70 days for a hearing, and you'll need to prepare evidence and appear. Recovery is enforceable but not guaranteed — judgments still need collecting.

In this scenario, the attorney letter is the highest expected-value step, and it's reversible — if it doesn't work, small claims is still on the table.

## When a letter is the right tool

If the amount in dispute is more than a few times the cost of a flat-fee letter, the debtor is reachable, and you have documentation, an attorney-signed demand letter is almost always the highest expected-value first step. It costs once, can be sent in a day, and resolves a large share of B2B disputes without anyone setting foot in court.
