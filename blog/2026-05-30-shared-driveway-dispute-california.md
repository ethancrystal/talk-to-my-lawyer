To resolve a shared driveway dispute in California, start by finding the recorded easement or agreement that defines each owner's rights, then address the specific problem — blocked access, parking, or repair costs — in a formal demand letter. Neither owner may unreasonably interfere with the other's use, and maintenance is generally shared in proportion to use.

A shared driveway is a daily necessity and, too often, a daily source of friction. Here is how the law sorts it out.

## How are shared driveway rights created?

Before you can resolve a dispute, you need to know the legal basis for the arrangement.

### A recorded easement

Most shared driveways are governed by an easement — a recorded right to use land for access. The driveway may sit entirely on one owner's parcel with the neighbor holding a right-of-way across it, or it may straddle the boundary with each owner granting the other reciprocal access. Your deed, title report, and the county recorder's records will reveal any recorded easement and, ideally, its terms.

### A shared driveway or maintenance agreement

Some properties come with a written shared driveway agreement that spells out who may use the driveway, how costs are split, parking rules, and dispute procedures. These agreements are valuable because they answer most questions before they arise. If one exists, it controls.

### An implied or prescriptive easement

If there is no recorded easement but a single driveway has long served two parcels that were once one, an implied easement may exist based on the prior use and the parties' presumed intent. Alternatively, if a neighbor has openly, continuously, and without permission used a driveway for five years, a prescriptive easement to continue that use may have formed. Both are harder to prove than a recorded easement and depend heavily on evidence.

## What can each owner do — and not do?

The defining feature of a shared driveway easement is that neither owner may unreasonably interfere with the other's use. In practice:

- **No blocking.** Neither party may park, store items, place trash cans, or install gates or structures that prevent the other from using the driveway for access.
- **Reasonable, shared use.** Both owners are entitled to reasonable use consistent with the easement's purpose — typically getting in and out (ingress and egress).
- **No overburdening.** An owner cannot expand the use beyond the easement's scope, for example by using a residential driveway for heavy commercial traffic or by parking numerous extra vehicles that crowd out the neighbor.

A recurring flashpoint is parking. Unless the easement or agreement expressly allows it, a right-of-way for *access* does not necessarily include the right to *park* on the shared driveway — especially where parking would block the neighbor. The specific terms (or the established practice in an implied or prescriptive easement) determine what parking is permitted.

## Who pays to maintain a shared driveway?

This is one of the most common disputes, and California law addresses it directly. When a private road or driveway easement is owned or used by more than one owner, the cost of maintaining, repairing, and restoring it is generally shared in proportion to each owner's use, unless the owners have agreed otherwise in writing.

That default rule means a neighbor usually cannot simply refuse to contribute to necessary repairs of a driveway they use. At the same time, an owner who wants to make repairs should communicate, propose a fair cost split based on use, and document the need and the estimates — rather than acting unilaterally and then demanding reimbursement. A written maintenance agreement that sets a clear formula avoids most of these conflicts.

## What are the most common shared driveway disputes?

- **Blocked access.** One neighbor parks on or obstructs the driveway, preventing the other from getting in or out.
- **Parking disagreements.** Disputes over whether, where, and how long each party may park on the shared surface.
- **Refusal to share repair costs.** One owner declines to contribute to repaving, drainage fixes, or other necessary maintenance.
- **Overuse or commercial use.** One household's traffic, guests, or business activity overwhelms the shared space.
- **Gates and improvements.** One owner installs a gate or structure that interferes with shared use.
- **Scope and boundary confusion.** The parties disagree about exactly where the easement runs or who owns the underlying land.

## What remedies are available?

If informal efforts fail, California law provides several remedies:

- **Injunction.** A court can order a neighbor to stop blocking the driveway, remove an obstruction or gate, or cease overburdening the easement.
- **Contribution for maintenance.** An owner who has paid for necessary repairs may seek a proportionate contribution from the other users based on their use.
- **Damages.** You may recover losses caused by the interference.
- **Quiet title or declaratory relief.** Where the existence, scope, or location of the easement is genuinely disputed, a court can declare the parties' rights, providing certainty going forward.

Because litigation over a driveway is expensive relative to the stakes, courts and parties alike strongly prefer negotiated solutions — which is exactly where a well-drafted demand letter is most useful.

## What are the steps to resolve it?

1. **Find the documents.** Obtain your deed, title report, recorded easement, and any shared driveway or maintenance agreement.
2. **Confirm the layout.** If the easement's location or width is unclear, a survey can settle it.
3. **Document the problem.** Photograph blocked access, improper parking, or disrepair, and keep a log of incidents and communications.
4. **Propose a written arrangement.** Where none exists, suggest a simple shared-use and maintenance agreement covering parking, costs, and dispute resolution.
5. **Send a demand letter.** A formal letter setting out the rights and demanding a specific change is often the turning point.
6. **Escalate only if needed.** If the neighbor refuses, injunctive relief or a contribution action may follow.

## Why send a demand letter?

Many neighbors continue problematic behavior simply because they do not understand the legal rights attached to the easement, or because verbal requests have been easy to ignore. An effective shared driveway demand letter identifies the easement or agreement and its source, describes the specific problem (blocking, improper parking, refusal to share repair costs, or overburdening), explains the relevant California rule, and demands a concrete remedy by a reasonable deadline. Where appropriate, it can also propose a written shared-use and cost-sharing arrangement to prevent future conflict.

Because the letter is firm, specific, and grounded in the documents and the law, it shows the neighbor you understand your rights and are prepared to enforce them — frequently prompting cooperation. It also builds a dated record of your good-faith effort to resolve the matter. The same approach makes [demand letters](/blog/2026-05-13-what-is-a-demand-letter) effective across property disputes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can my neighbor park on our shared driveway?
It depends on the easement or agreement. A right-of-way for access does not automatically include the right to park, particularly where parking blocks your use. Check the recorded terms or, for an implied or prescriptive easement, the established practice.

### My neighbor won't help pay for repairs. Do they have to?
Usually, yes. California law generally requires owners who share a private road or driveway easement to contribute to maintenance and repair in proportion to their use, unless they have agreed otherwise. Communicate, document the need, and propose a fair split.

### Can I install a gate on the shared driveway?
Only if it does not unreasonably interfere with your neighbor's use. A gate that blocks or impairs the neighbor's access generally is not allowed without their agreement, even if it sits on your portion of the land.

### What if there's no written agreement at all?
You may still have an implied or prescriptive easement based on long-standing use, and the default proportional-maintenance rule may apply. Because these are harder to prove, documentation of the history of use is essential — old photos, witness statements, and a timeline of how the driveway has been used. Creating a written agreement going forward is wise to prevent the next dispute.

### How do I find out my driveway rights?
Start with your deed and a title report, and search the county recorder's records for any recorded easement or shared driveway agreement. A survey can clarify the physical layout if the boundary or easement width is unclear.

## How can neighbors prevent shared driveway disputes?

The most reliable way to avoid a shared driveway conflict is to put the arrangement in writing before problems arise. A simple shared-use and maintenance agreement can specify who may use the driveway and for what, where and when parking is allowed, how repair and maintenance costs are split (and how decisions about major work are made), and a basic process for resolving disagreements. Recording the agreement so it binds future owners adds long-term certainty, sparing the next buyers the same friction. Even an informal but signed written understanding is far better than relying on memory and goodwill. When both households know the rules, the everyday give-and-take of sharing a driveway becomes routine rather than a recurring source of tension — and if a dispute does arise, the written terms make the answer clear and a demand letter easy to support.

Shared driveways force two households to cooperate over the same essential space. When goodwill runs out, the law steps in — and a clear, well-supported demand letter, often paired with a simple written agreement for the future, resolves most disputes without a lawsuit.
