# The California Contractor's Complete Guide to Getting Paid in 2026: Liens, Prompt Payment, and Demand Letters

**Short answer:** California contractors have a layered toolkit for collecting payment — preliminary notices to preserve lien rights, demand letters to apply early pressure, mechanics liens to cloud title, prompt-payment penalties of up to 2% per month, and small claims or civil court as the backstop. Using them in the right order, while protecting strict deadlines, is what gets you paid.

Doing excellent work is only half the job. The other half — actually collecting what you're owed — sinks more California construction businesses than any tool failure or bad weather ever could. The good news is that California law gives contractors more leverage than almost any other state, if you understand the system and respect its deadlines. This guide lays out the whole playbook for 2026.

## Why is getting paid so hard in construction?

Construction payment moves down a chain: owner pays the general contractor, the GC pays subcontractors, subs pay suppliers and laborers. Every link is a place where money can stall — a disputed change order, a cash-flow squeeze, a client who decides to "renegotiate" after the work is finished. Because the money is tied to real property, California built an entire body of law — the mechanics lien system — specifically to protect the people who improve that property. Your job is to use it.

## Step 1: Protect your lien rights before there's a problem

The single most important thing you can do happens at the **start** of a job, not the end: serve a **preliminary notice** (often called the "20-day notice").

Under California Civil Code § 8200, most subcontractors, suppliers, and laborers must serve a preliminary notice within 20 days of first furnishing labor or materials to preserve their right to record a mechanics lien later. Miss it, and you can lose lien rights entirely for work outside that 20-day window. Direct (prime) contractors who deal straight with the owner have somewhat different requirements, but serving notice is still good practice.

A preliminary notice is not an accusation — it's routine paperwork that simply says you're working on the property and reserving your rights. Treat it as a standard part of starting every job.

## Step 2: Get the contract right (and keep your license current)

Two foundation issues decide whether you can collect at all:

**Your license.** Anyone performing construction on a project of $500 or more in labor and materials must be licensed by the Contractors State License Board (Business & Professions Code § 7048 sets the narrow minor-work exemption). Under Business & Professions Code § 7031, an **unlicensed** contractor generally cannot sue to collect compensation — and the client can even seek to recover everything already paid. Confirm your license was valid and in the right classification for every day of the project.

**The written contract.** For home improvement work, California requires a written contract with specific disclosures (Business & Professions Code § 7159), and it caps the **down payment** at the lesser of 10% of the contract price or $1,000. A compliant contract, signed change orders, and clear progress-billing terms are your best evidence when a client disputes the final invoice.

## Step 3: Send a demand letter before you escalate

When payment stalls, a written demand letter is almost always the right first move. It's fast, inexpensive, and resolves a large share of disputes without recording a lien or filing suit.

A strong contractor demand letter:

- Identifies the project, contract, and exact amount owed.
- Attaches proof of completion — photos, sign-offs, inspection approvals, invoices.
- Notes that written contracts carry a **four-year statute of limitations** in California (Code of Civil Procedure § 337), so delay won't help the client.
- Claims **10% annual prejudgment interest** on the liquidated debt (Civil Code § 3289).
- Sets a firm deadline (commonly 10 days) and states the next step: a mechanics lien and collection action.

An attorney-signed letter raises the stakes without committing you to litigation. For the anatomy of an effective one, see [what is a demand letter for unpaid invoices](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices).

## Step 4: Use the Notice of Intent to Lien as a bridge

If the demand letter alone doesn't work, escalate with a [Notice of Intent to Lien](/blog/2026-06-02-what-is-a-notice-of-intent-to-lien-california) — a written warning that you will record a mechanics lien unless paid by a deadline. California doesn't require it before a standard lien, but it's effective: owners and GCs know a recorded lien clouds title and can derail a sale or refinance, so many pay at this stage to avoid it.

## Step 5: Record the mechanics lien — and mind the clock

A mechanics lien attaches to the property's title and is your heaviest leverage. The deadlines are unforgiving:

- **Record within your window.** A direct contractor generally must record within 90 days after the work is complete, or 60 days after the owner records a **Notice of Completion** (Civil Code § 8412–8414). Subcontractors have parallel deadlines.
- **Sue to foreclose within 90 days of recording.** If you don't file a foreclosure action within 90 days, the lien becomes unenforceable (Civil Code § 8460). You can extend by recording a notice if the owner agrees, but don't count on it.
- **Release promptly when paid.** Once you're paid, record a release — California penalizes claimants who wrongfully refuse to release a satisfied or expired lien (Civil Code § 8480 and § 8482).

Because the timing is so strict, this is the stage where contractors most often benefit from counsel: a missed deadline can erase your strongest remedy.

## Step 6: Know the stop payment notice and payment bond options

A mechanics lien isn't the only device. A **stop payment notice** (Civil Code § 8500 and following) lets a claimant intercept undisbursed construction funds held by the owner or lender — useful when there's still money in the project to reach. On bonded jobs, you may also have a claim against a **payment bond**. These tools can reach money even when a lien against title is impractical. The right combination depends on your role in the chain and who's holding the funds — explored further in [mechanics lien vs. stop notice vs. demand letter vs. small claims](/blog/2026-06-02-mechanics-lien-vs-stop-notice-vs-demand-letter-vs-small-claims-california).

## Step 7: Invoke prompt-payment penalties

California's prompt-payment laws put a price on stalling. When a prime contractor wrongfully withholds payment from a subcontractor, Business & Professions Code § 7108.5 can impose a penalty of **2% per month** on the wrongfully withheld amount, plus attorney's fees in an action to collect. Private-works progress and retention payments have their own timelines and penalties (Civil Code § 8800 and following), and public-works projects add another layer. When these apply, cite them — they convert "I'll get to it" into a measurable, growing liability for the other side.

## Step 8: Small claims or civil court as the backstop

If letters and liens don't resolve it, court is the backstop:

- **Small claims** handles individual claims up to **$12,500** (business entities are limited to a lower amount). It's fast, inexpensive, and lawyer-free — well suited to clean payment disputes with good documentation.
- **Civil court** handles larger claims and lien foreclosures. It's slower and costlier, but the credible threat of filing — backed by a recorded lien and a clear paper trail — settles most disputes before trial.

## What's the right order of operations?

Preserve lien rights at the start → confirm your license and contract → send a demand letter → escalate with a Notice of Intent to Lien → record the lien within the deadline → layer in stop notices, bond claims, and prompt-pay penalties where they apply → file in small claims or civil court only if you must. Each step costs more than the last, so the discipline is to start early and escalate only as needed.

## Frequently asked questions

**How long do I have to record a mechanics lien in California?** Generally 90 days after completion for a direct contractor, or 60 days after the owner records a Notice of Completion. Subcontractors have parallel windows. Confirm your specific dates — the deadlines are strict.

**Can I charge interest on a late payment?** Yes — California allows 10% annual prejudgment interest on a liquidated contract debt (Civil Code § 3289), and prompt-payment statutes can add penalties on top.

**Do I need a lawyer to send a demand letter?** No, but an attorney-signed letter that cites the right statutes and deadlines carries more weight and reduces the risk of waiving a right by accident. Many contractors use a flat-fee letter as a low-cost first step.

**What if I never sent a preliminary notice?** You may have limited or no lien rights as a sub or supplier, but you can still pursue a demand letter and a breach of contract claim. Talk to counsel about your specific timeline.

Getting paid in California construction is a system, not a single move. Run the system in order, respect every deadline, and you'll collect far more — and far faster — than contractors who wait until the job is over to start thinking about it.
