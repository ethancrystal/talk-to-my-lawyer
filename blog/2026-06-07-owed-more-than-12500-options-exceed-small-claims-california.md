# Owed More Than $12,500? Your Options When a California Dispute Exceeds the Small Claims Limit

**Short answer: If you're owed more than $12,500 in California, you can't use small claims court without waiving the excess. Your realistic options are: (1) waive down to $12,500 and file small claims, (2) file a limited civil case for up to $35,000 in Superior Court, (3) file an unlimited civil case for any amount, or (4) pursue private arbitration if your contract requires it. A demand letter is still your best first move regardless of the amount.**

Small claims court is the people's court — no lawyers, fast hearings, $75 filing fee. But it has a hard ceiling: $12,500 for individual plaintiffs under CCP § 116.221 (and just $6,250 for businesses). When your claim exceeds that number, you're suddenly in a different legal universe where attorneys get involved, discovery is permitted, and timelines stretch from weeks to months or years.

The good news: you still have options that don't require mortgaging your house to fund litigation.

## Option 1: Waive the Excess and File Small Claims Anyway

This sounds counterintuitive, but it's often the smartest move.

**How it works:** CCP § 116.220(b) allows you to file in small claims for $12,500 even if your actual damages are higher. You permanently waive everything above $12,500.

**When this makes sense:**
- Your claim is $13,000-$18,000. The amount you'd recover in Superior Court (after attorney fees) might be less than $12,500 anyway.
- The debtor has limited assets. Collecting $12,500 from someone who can barely pay that is better than getting a $20,000 judgment you can't enforce.
- Speed matters more than maximum recovery. Small claims resolves in 30-70 days. Superior Court takes 12-18 months.

**The math:** If you're owed $16,000 and an attorney would cost $5,000-$10,000 in Superior Court fees and legal costs, you net $6,000-$11,000. Filing small claims for $12,500 costs $75 and a few hours. You net $12,425.

| Factor | Waive to Small Claims | Pursue Full Amount |
|--------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Filing cost | $75 | $435-$550+ |
| Attorney fees | $0 (prohibited) | $5,000-$25,000+ |
| Timeline | 30-70 days | 12-24 months |
| Maximum recovery | $12,500 | Unlimited |
| Appeals | Limited | Full |
| Discovery | None | Yes |

**Statute:** CCP § 116.220-116.221 governs small claims jurisdictional limits. The $12,500 cap applies per plaintiff per claim — you cannot split one dispute into multiple filings (CCP § 116.420).

## What if I'm Owed $12,501-$35,000? The Limited Civil Case

California has a middle path most people don't know about: the limited civil case.

**Jurisdictional range:** $12,500.01 to $35,000 (CCP § 85).

**What makes it different from unlimited civil:**
- Simplified discovery rules (CCP § 94-95)
- Economic litigation procedures available (CCP § 90-98)
- Shorter trial times — courts prioritize these cases
- Lower filing fees ($435 vs. $550+)
- Limited to three depositions per side
- 30 interrogatories maximum (vs. 35 in unlimited)

**What makes it different from small claims:**
- Attorneys ARE allowed (and often recommended)
- Full collection tools available if you win
- Discovery permitted — you can force the other side to produce documents
- Jury trial available upon request
- Appeals go to the appellate division of Superior Court

**Filing fee:** $435 in most California counties (check your local court — some vary slightly).

**Best for:** Contract disputes, unpaid invoices, security deposit claims, and property damage cases in the $13,000-$35,000 range where you have documentation and the other side has assets worth pursuing.

> **Statute callout:** Under CCP § 85(a), the amount in controversy for a limited civil case includes damages demanded, exclusive of interest, attorney's fees, and costs. If your complaint seeks $35,001 or more, you must file as unlimited.

## What About Claims Over $35,000? The Unlimited Civil Case

This is full-scale litigation. Everything you've heard about lawsuits being expensive and slow? This is where that reputation comes from.

**Jurisdictional range:** Over $35,000 — no ceiling (CCP § 88).

**What you get:**
- Unlimited damages — pursue the full amount
- Full discovery tools (depositions, interrogatories, requests for production, subpoenas)
- Jury trial rights
- Ability to seek punitive damages (in appropriate cases)
- Equitable remedies (injunctions, specific performance)
- Attorney fee shifting available in contract cases (if the contract includes a fee-shifting clause — Civil Code § 1717)

**What you pay:**
- Filing fee: $550+ (first paper filed)
- Attorney fees: $10,000-$50,000+ for straightforward cases; six figures for complex ones
- Expert witness fees: $5,000-$25,000+ per expert
- Deposition costs: $1,000-$5,000+ per deposition
- Timeline: 18-36 months to trial (often longer)
- Emotional cost: depositions, document production, court appearances

**Realistic cost-benefit analysis:** Most attorneys recommend unlimited civil only when:
- The claim exceeds $50,000 (the economics start working at this threshold)
- The defendant has assets or insurance to satisfy a judgment
- You have strong documentary evidence
- The case involves a fee-shifting provision

## Option 4: Private Arbitration or Mediation

If your contract includes an arbitration clause — and most commercial contracts in California do — you may be required to arbitrate regardless of the amount.

**Arbitration advantages:**
- Faster than court (typically 6-12 months)
- Private — no public record
- Limited discovery keeps costs lower than unlimited civil
- Binding — the award is enforceable like a judgment
- Arbitrator is often a subject-matter expert

**Arbitration disadvantages:**
- Filing fees can be steep ($1,500-$10,000+ depending on the amount in dispute and the arbitration forum)
- Limited appeal rights — you're essentially stuck with the arbitrator's decision
- No jury
- If the arbitration clause is in a contract of adhesion, you may be able to challenge it (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, 24 Cal.4th 83)

**Mediation vs. Arbitration:** Mediation is non-binding negotiation with a neutral facilitator. It's a step before arbitration or litigation, not a replacement. Most California courts require mediation before trial (Local Rules vary by county). Average mediation cost: $2,000-$5,000 for a half-day session.

> **Timeline comparison:**
> - Small claims: 30-70 days
> - Mediation: 2-6 weeks to schedule, 1 day to complete
> - Limited civil: 9-15 months
> - Arbitration: 6-12 months
> - Unlimited civil: 18-36 months

## Why a Demand Letter Is Still Step One — Regardless of the Amount

Here's the thing nobody tells you about disputes over $12,500: most of them still settle before anyone files anything. The filing fee isn't the leverage. The demand letter is.

A well-crafted demand letter does three things simultaneously:

1. **Establishes the paper trail.** If you later file in Superior Court, you want to show the court (and a jury) that you tried to resolve this reasonably.

2. **Triggers the cost calculation.** When the other side receives a letter that clearly articulates the legal basis for your claim, cites the relevant statutes, and sets a deadline — they consult an attorney. That attorney tells them what defending your case will cost. For most disputes, the defense cost exceeds the disputed amount.

3. **Creates urgency without creating hostility.** Filing a lawsuit makes people defensive. A demand letter gives them a graceful exit.

**Settlement rates:** Industry data consistently shows that 60-70% of disputes resolve after a properly drafted demand letter, without any court filing. For amounts between $12,500 and $50,000, that percentage is even higher — the amount is too large to ignore but often not large enough to justify full litigation costs for either side.

Under CCP § 1021.5, courts may award attorney fees to parties who enforce important public rights. But even without fee-shifting, the economics of a demand letter are straightforward: $300-$500 for a professional letter versus $10,000+ for litigation.

## Decision Tree: Which Option Is Right for Your Dispute?

Ask yourself these questions in order:

**1. Is the other party likely to pay voluntarily if confronted with a formal demand?**
- Yes → Send a demand letter first. You'll likely resolve this without filing anything.
- No / Unsure → Continue to question 2.

**2. How much are you actually owed?**
- $12,501-$12,500 (after considering: would you accept $12,500?) → Waive to small claims.
- $12,501-$35,000 → Limited civil case (after demand letter).
- Over $35,000 → Unlimited civil case (after demand letter and possibly mediation).

**3. Does your contract have an arbitration clause?**
- Yes → You likely must arbitrate regardless of amount.
- No → Continue to appropriate court filing.

**4. Does the other party have assets to satisfy a judgment?**
- Yes → Pursuing the full amount makes sense.
- No / Unknown → Consider waiving to small claims — a $12,500 judgment you can collect beats a $50,000 judgment you can't.

**5. Is speed or maximum recovery more important?**
- Speed → Small claims or arbitration.
- Maximum recovery → Limited or unlimited civil.

## What About California's New SB 555? (Small Claims Limit Increases)

California legislators have periodically proposed raising the small claims limit. SB 555 and similar bills have sought to increase the cap to $25,000 for individuals. As of 2026, the limit remains $12,500 for individuals and $6,250 for businesses (CCP § 116.221).

If legislation passes, it would expand access to the no-attorney, low-cost small claims process for a much larger range of disputes. Until then, the options outlined above remain your toolkit.

**Practical tip:** Even if legislation is pending, don't wait. Statutes of limitations keep running (see our guide on [how long you have to sue in California](/blog/how-long-to-sue-california-statutes-of-limitations-2026)). File based on current law, not hoped-for changes.

## How Collection Works After You Win

Winning is only half the battle. Collecting the judgment is the other half — and it's often harder.

**Small claims collection tools:**
- Wage garnishment (up to 25% of disposable earnings — CCP § 706.050)
- Bank levy (CCP § 700.140)
- Property lien (abstract of judgment)
- Debtor's examination (CCP § 708.110)

**Superior Court (limited and unlimited civil) adds:**
- More aggressive discovery in aid of execution
- Till-tap orders for business debtors
- Appointment of a receiver
- Charging orders against LLC interests

**Key statute:** California judgments are enforceable for 10 years and renewable for another 10 (CCP § 683.020). Time is on your side if the debtor doesn't have assets now but might later.
