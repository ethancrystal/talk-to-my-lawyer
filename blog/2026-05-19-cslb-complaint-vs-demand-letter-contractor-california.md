# Filing a CSLB Complaint vs. Sending a Demand Letter: Which Gets Your Money Back Faster in California?

If getting your money back quickly is the priority, a demand letter is faster. CSLB complaints take 60–90 days for initial response and can stretch much longer. A demand letter typically produces a response within 15–30 days. But the smartest approach in most California contractor disputes is to do both — the demand letter for speed and the CSLB complaint for leverage.

These two tools solve different problems. Understanding that difference is the key to using them effectively.

## How Fast Does Each Option Actually Work?

A demand letter sets its own timeline. You choose the deadline — usually 15 to 30 days — and the contractor must decide whether to respond before that deadline passes. If they respond, you're in negotiation. If they don't, you have documented evidence of their refusal for your next step.

A CSLB complaint moves on the state's timeline. After you file under Business & Professions Code §7011, the CSLB assigns an investigator, contacts the contractor, and may schedule an inspection. The investigation alone can take three to six months. If the CSLB orders restitution, enforcement adds more time.

Speed comparison: demand letter wins by a significant margin. But speed isn't the only factor.

## What Can Each One Actually Accomplish?

A demand letter can recover money. It can compel a contractor to finish work, make repairs, or refund what you paid. It creates a legal record showing you attempted good-faith resolution before escalating. What it cannot do is threaten someone's professional license — that's purely a CSLB function.

A CSLB complaint can result in license suspension or revocation under B&P Code §7106, ordered restitution, and even criminal referral under B&P Code §7028.1 for serious violations. What it cannot do is guarantee you'll get paid quickly, because the CSLB's enforcement process has its own pace.

This is exactly why pairing the two makes sense. The demand letter creates urgency. The CSLB complaint creates existential threat to the contractor's business. Together, they cover both the speed and leverage dimensions.

## Which One Costs More?

A CSLB complaint is free. You file it online or by mail and the state investigates at no cost to you.

So the cost comparison favors doing both: the complaint costs nothing and the demand letter is either free or affordable. There's no reason to choose one over the other when you can run them simultaneously.

## When Should You Skip One and Just Do the Other?

Skip the CSLB complaint if the contractor is unlicensed — the CSLB has no jurisdiction over them. Instead, send a demand letter citing B&P Code §7031(b), which lets you recover all compensation paid to an unlicensed contractor, and head to small claims court if they don't pay.

Skip the demand letter only if you've already communicated your demands clearly in writing (emails or texts can count) and the contractor has explicitly refused. In that case, go straight to the CSLB complaint and small claims.

But in most situations involving a licensed contractor who owes you money for [shoddy work](/blog/2026-05-19-contractor-did-shoddy-work-refuses-fix-california) or an abandoned project, doing both is the right call. The demand letter moves fast. The CSLB complaint adds pressure that makes the demand letter more effective.

## What's the Bottom Line?

Use a demand letter when you want your money back quickly. Use a CSLB complaint when you want accountability and long-term leverage. Use both when you want the best chance of resolution without going to court.
