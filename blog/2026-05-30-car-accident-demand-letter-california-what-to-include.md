# Car Accident Demand Letter in California: What to Include and How Much to Ask For (2026 Guide)

**Short Answer:** A car accident demand letter is a written request to the at-fault driver's insurer for compensation. It should include the facts, liability, your medical treatment and bills, lost wages, pain and suffering, and a specific dollar demand. In California, your recovery is reduced by your share of fault under pure comparative negligence.

After a collision, the demand letter is the centerpiece of settling your claim without a lawsuit. A clear, well-documented letter tells the insurer you understand your damages and your rights — and it anchors the negotiation around your number instead of theirs. This guide explains exactly what to include and how to decide how much to ask for in 2026.

## When should you send a car accident demand letter?

Timing matters. The best practice is to wait until you've reached "maximum medical improvement" — the point where you've recovered or your doctors can describe your lasting condition. Sending a demand before you know the full extent of your injuries risks under-valuing the claim, because once you settle, you generally can't reopen it for later-discovered harm. Send it after treatment stabilizes but well before the statute of limitations runs.

## What is the deadline to act in California?

California gives you **two years** from the date of the accident to file a personal injury lawsuit, under Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1. For property damage (your vehicle), the limit is generally **three years** under § 338. Claims against a government entity are far shorter — usually a six-month claim-filing deadline. A demand letter doesn't pause these clocks, so never let negotiations push you past your filing deadline.

## How does comparative fault affect what you can recover?

California follows **pure comparative negligence**, established in *Li v. Yellow Cab Co.* Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault — but you can recover even if you were mostly at fault. If your damages are $50,000 and you're found 20% responsible, you recover $40,000. Insurers use comparative fault aggressively to shrink payouts, so your demand letter should address liability head-on and explain why the other driver bears the responsibility.

## What should a car accident demand letter include?

A persuasive letter is organized and evidence-backed. Include:

1. **The parties and claim details** — your information, the at-fault driver, the insurer, the claim number, and the date and location of the accident.
2. **A factual narrative** — a clear, chronological account of how the collision happened, written to establish the other driver's liability.
3. **Liability analysis** — why the other driver was at fault, supported by the police report, witness statements, photos, and any traffic-law violations.
4. **Your injuries and treatment** — a description of your injuries, the treatment you received, and your prognosis.
5. **An itemized damages calculation** — every category of loss with documentation.
6. **A specific demand amount** — the total you're seeking.
7. **A response deadline** — commonly 30 days.

Attach copies of the police report, medical records and bills, wage-loss documentation, and photos.

## How much should you ask for?

Your demand is built from two kinds of damages:

**Economic (special) damages** are your measurable, out-of-pocket losses:

- **Medical expenses** — past bills and the reasonable cost of future treatment.
- **Lost wages** — income you missed during recovery, plus any reduced future earning capacity.
- **Property damage** — vehicle repair or replacement, plus related costs like a rental car.
- **Other out-of-pocket costs** — prescriptions, medical devices, mileage to appointments.

**Non-economic (general) damages** compensate for harms without a receipt:

- **Pain and suffering**
- **Emotional distress**
- **Loss of enjoyment of life**
- **Disfigurement or permanent disability**

There's no rigid formula California courts require, but adjusters and attorneys often estimate general damages as a multiple of your economic damages — a higher multiplier for severe, lasting injuries and a lower one for minor, fully healed ones. Whatever method you use, your letter should justify the number with the facts of your injury, not assert it out of thin air.

A practical tactic: set your opening demand somewhat above your true settlement target, because the insurer will almost certainly counter low. Leave room to negotiate toward a fair figure without starting below it.

## Should you mention policy limits and interest?

Know the at-fault driver's policy limits, because they cap what that policy will pay. If your damages exceed the limits, you may need to pursue your own underinsured motorist coverage. You can also note that California allows prejudgment interest in certain circumstances and that an unreasonable refusal to settle a clear claim can carry consequences for the insurer. Keep these references measured and factual.

## Common mistakes that weaken a car accident demand

- **Demanding too early.** Sending before your injuries stabilize almost always under-values the claim.
- **Exaggerating injuries or damages.** Inflated claims destroy credibility and invite scrutiny of everything else.
- **Ignoring comparative fault.** If you don't address liability, the insurer will assign you as much fault as it can.
- **Leaving out documentation.** An unsupported number is easy to discount. Records are leverage.
- **Missing the statute of limitations.** Negotiation doesn't extend your deadline to sue.
- **Accepting the first offer.** Opening offers are usually low; the demand letter starts a negotiation, not a take-it-or-leave-it.

## What happens after you send it?

The insurer typically responds within a few weeks with an acceptance, a counteroffer, or a denial. A counteroffer is normal and starts the back-and-forth toward settlement. Keep every exchange in writing, stay anchored to your documented damages, and don't let the process drift past your filing deadline. If the insurer won't deal fairly, your well-built letter becomes the foundation for a lawsuit. To understand the broader litigation path, see [how to sue someone who owes you money in California](/blog/2026-05-28-how-to-sue-someone-who-owes-you-money-california).

## Should an attorney write it?

For minor property-only claims, many people handle the demand themselves. But for injury claims — especially with significant medical treatment, disputed liability, or lasting harm — an attorney-signed demand letter usually produces a higher settlement and signals you're prepared to litigate. Many personal injury attorneys work on contingency, and a flat-fee attorney letter is an option for those who want professional drafting without a full representation agreement.

## The bottom line

A strong California car accident demand letter is patient and precise: it waits until your injuries stabilize, establishes the other driver's liability, itemizes your economic and non-economic damages with documentation, accounts for comparative fault, and names a specific number with room to negotiate. Build it on evidence, mind your two-year deadline, and you give the insurer every reason to settle fairly — and a clean record if it won't.

## A worked example of how the number comes together

Imagine a rear-end collision where the other driver was clearly at fault. Your economic damages are $12,000 in medical bills, $3,000 in lost wages, and $6,000 in vehicle repair — $21,000 total. Your injuries involved months of physical therapy but a full recovery. Using a moderate multiplier of two for general damages on the injury-related economic losses ($15,000 × 2 = $30,000), your total claim might be framed around **$51,000**.

If the insurer argues you were 10% at fault for following too closely, pure comparative negligence would reduce a $51,000 result to about $45,900. Knowing that in advance, you might open your demand somewhat higher to preserve room to settle near your true target. The point isn't the exact figure — it's that every component is built from documented losses and a defensible method, so the insurer can't dismiss the number as arbitrary.

## How to handle the back-and-forth after your demand

Most claims settle through negotiation, not the first response. When the insurer counters low, don't react emotionally — ask, in writing, for the specific reasons behind their figure. If they're discounting your medical treatment, point them back to the records; if they're assigning you fault, walk them through the liability evidence. Make incremental, justified moves toward your target rather than dramatic swings. Keep every offer and counter in writing, and never feel pressured to accept a quick settlement before you understand the full extent of your injuries. If talks stall and your deadline approaches, be prepared to file suit to preserve your rights — a credible willingness to litigate is often what moves the final offer.

## Should you give a recorded statement first?

Insurers sometimes ask for a recorded statement before responding to your demand. You're generally not required to give one to the *other* driver's insurer, and casual answers can be used to minimize your claim. It's wise to be cautious, stick to documented facts, and consider speaking with an attorney before agreeing to any recorded interview about how the accident happened or the extent of your injuries.
