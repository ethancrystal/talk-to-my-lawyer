# I'm Owed Money But Don't Want to Sue — What Are My Real Options in California?

You're owed money. The deadline came and went. You've called, you've emailed, and now you're staring at the word "lawsuit" wondering if there's another way. There is.

**Short answer:** Before suing, California gives you four practical options: a final written demand from you, an attorney-signed demand letter, a small claims filing (up to $12,500 for individuals), or private mediation. Most disputes under $10,000 settle after a single attorney demand letter — which costs far less than filing fees, court dates, and the months you'd lose to litigation.

This article walks through each option in order of cost, then helps you decide which one fits your situation.

## What's the cheapest option, and does it ever work?

The cheapest option is a clean, dated final invoice that includes three things: the original amount owed, a specific payment deadline (typically 7–14 days), and a clear statement of what happens next if the deadline passes. Send it by email and certified mail.

This works more often than people expect — but only when the other side actually believes you'll follow through. Most non-payers have already ignored several reminders by the time you reach this step. If you've already sent multiple casual follow-ups, your own letter has lost its weight. The next message has to come from someone the debtor takes seriously.

That's where an attorney-signed letter changes the dynamic.

## When should you skip ahead to a lawyer-signed demand letter?

A [demand letter](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices) on attorney letterhead signals three things the debtor can't ignore: a lawyer has reviewed your claim, the amount and legal basis have been documented, and litigation is the realistic next step. In California, the four-year statute of limitations on written contracts (Code of Civil Procedure § 337) means the debtor can't simply wait you out.

Skip ahead to an attorney letter when:

- You're owed more than about $500–$1,000 (below that, the math gets tighter)
- The debtor is a business that cares about its reputation
- You've already sent your own reminders and been ignored
- The relationship is over and you're focused on recovery, not future work

Attorney letters from flat-fee services typically cost a small fraction of what you're owed. If the letter prompts payment — and they often do for B2B disputes — you've resolved the matter without ever filing in court.

## Is small claims really an alternative — or just a slower version of the same thing?

California small claims court allows individuals to sue for up to $12,500, and sole proprietors and businesses for up to $6,250 per claim (with limits on how many large claims you can file per year). Filing fees range from $30 to $75, and you don't need a lawyer — in fact, attorneys aren't allowed to represent parties at the initial hearing.

Small claims is genuinely useful when the dispute is straightforward, the amount fits, and you've exhausted softer steps. But it isn't a shortcut. You'll typically wait 30–70 days for a hearing, you'll need to prepare your evidence, and you'll have to show up — possibly more than once if the other side requests a continuance. Winning a judgment is also not the same as getting paid; collection is a separate process.

Many Californians use small claims and an attorney demand letter together: send the letter first, then file in small claims if it's ignored. The letter alone often makes the filing unnecessary.

## What if mediation makes more sense than any letter?

Mediation is worth considering when there's a continuing relationship to preserve, when the dispute is partly about something other than money (deliverables, scope, expectations), or when both sides agree the facts are murky. A private mediator can run a structured conversation for a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, depending on the venue and the size of the dispute. Many California counties also offer free or low-cost community mediation programs.

Mediation is not a fit when one side denies owing anything, refuses to engage, or has gone silent. In those cases, a written demand reasserts the claim and creates a record — a step mediators typically expect you to have taken first.

## When a letter is the right tool

For most California disputes between $500 and $25,000 — unpaid invoices, broken contracts, returned-but-unrefunded goods, deposits not returned — a single attorney-signed demand letter resolves more matters than any other tool short of a lawsuit, at a fraction of the cost.
