# How Long Do You Have to Sue in California? Statutes of Limitations for Every Common Dispute (2026 Guide)

**Short answer: It depends on the type of claim. Written contracts: 4 years (CCP § 337). Oral contracts: 2 years (CCP § 339). Personal injury: 2 years (CCP § 335.1). Property damage: 3 years (CCP § 338). Fraud: 3 years from discovery (CCP § 338(d)). Missing the deadline is fatal — the court will dismiss your case regardless of how strong it is.**

The statute of limitations is the single most unforgiving rule in California law. You can have a slam-dunk case — clear liability, perfect documentation, a defendant with deep pockets — and lose everything because you filed one day late.

Courts don't have discretion here. If the defendant raises the statute of limitations as a defense and the clock has run, your case is over. No exceptions for "fairness" or "obvious liability."

That's why understanding your deadline — and acting well before it arrives — is more important than any other legal strategy.

## When Does the Clock Start? The "Accrual" Rules

The statute of limitations begins running on the date the cause of action "accrues." In plain language: the date you knew (or should have known) you had a claim.

**For most cases, accrual is straightforward:**
- Contract breach: the date the breach occurred (the missed payment date, the date of non-performance)
- Personal injury: the date of the injury
- Property damage: the date the damage happened

**But the "discovery rule" changes everything for certain claims:**

Under the discovery rule, the clock doesn't start until you discover (or reasonably should have discovered) the facts constituting the claim. This applies to:
- Fraud (CCP § 338(d)) — starts when you discover the fraud
- Medical malpractice (CCP § 340.5) — starts when you discover the injury
- Legal malpractice (CCP § 340.6) — starts when you discover or should have discovered the negligence
- Latent construction defects — starts when the defect manifests

> **Statute callout:** Under CCP § 338(d), fraud claims accrue upon "discovery by the aggrieved party of the facts constituting the fraud." The burden is on the plaintiff to show why they didn't discover it earlier. Courts apply a "reasonable person" standard — if a reasonable person would have investigated and discovered the fraud earlier, that earlier date controls.

## What "Tolling" Means and When It Applies

"Tolling" means the clock pauses. Several circumstances can toll California's statute of limitations:

**Defendant leaves California (CCP § 351).** If the person you want to sue leaves the state, the clock pauses for the time they're absent. This prevents defendants from evading lawsuits by crossing state lines. However, this tolling provision has limited application when the defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction in California despite being out of state (Practically speaking: if you can serve them out of state, the clock may still run).

**Plaintiff is a minor (CCP § 352).** Minors have until their 18th birthday plus the standard limitations period.

**Plaintiff is incapacitated (CCP § 352).** If the plaintiff lacks legal capacity (due to mental illness, imprisonment), the clock pauses.

**Defendant conceals themselves (CCP § 351).** If the defendant actively hides to avoid service of process.

**COVID-19 emergency rules.** During 2020-2021, various California court orders tolled statutes of limitations. These are largely expired but may affect older claims.

**Equitable tolling.** Courts may equitably toll the statute when:
- The plaintiff pursued the wrong remedy in good faith (e.g., filed an administrative complaint first)
- The plaintiff was induced to delay by the defendant's conduct
- The plaintiff filed a timely complaint but it was dismissed for procedural reasons

**Cross-complaints (CCP § 426.75).** If someone sues you and you have a compulsory cross-complaint, the statute is tolled while the original action is pending.

## Does a Demand Letter Toll the Statute of Limitations?

**No.** This is one of the most dangerous misconceptions. Sending a demand letter does not pause, extend, or reset the statute of limitations in California. The clock keeps running regardless of whether you've sent one letter, five letters, or none.

However, a demand letter serves a critical purpose even with the clock ticking:
- It may resolve the dispute before you need to file (60-70% of disputes settle after a demand letter)
- It creates evidence of the claim for later litigation
- It demonstrates reasonableness to a future court or jury
- Under CCP § 1021.5, it establishes that you attempted resolution before burdening the court system

**Strategic timing:** If your limitations period has 6+ months remaining, send the demand letter, give 30 days for response, then negotiate. If you're within 6 months of the deadline, send the demand letter AND prepare to file simultaneously. If you're within 30 days, file first and negotiate after — you can always dismiss the case voluntarily if settlement is reached.

## The Most Dangerous Scenarios: When People Miss Their Deadlines

**Scenario 1: "I thought we had a written contract."** You signed something, but it turns out the key term was agreed to verbally. That drops you from 4 years (CCP § 337) to 2 years (CCP § 339). If you're in year 3, you've already missed your window.

**Scenario 2: "They kept promising to pay."** Ongoing promises don't restart the clock. The statute began running on the original breach date. Partial payments may create a new promise (and a new deadline) under certain circumstances, but verbal promises alone typically don't.

**Scenario 3: "I didn't know I could sue for that."** Ignorance of your legal rights doesn't toll the statute. The clock started when you knew the facts — not when you learned those facts gave rise to a legal claim.

**Scenario 4: "I filed a complaint with an agency."** Filing an administrative complaint (with DFEH, OSHA, or a regulatory body) may toll the statute for that particular claim under specific conditions — but check carefully. Not all agency complaints toll, and the tolling often has a cap.

**Scenario 5: "The damage just got worse."** In continuing-harm situations (like ongoing trespass or nuisance), the statute may renew for new harm — but prior harm that occurred more than [X] years ago is still time-barred.

## Written vs. Oral Contracts: Why the Distinction Matters

The difference between 4 years and 2 years — a factor that's entirely within your control going forward:

**What counts as "written":**
- A signed contract
- A written agreement confirmed by email (under UETA — Civil Code § 1633.7)
- A purchase order that was accepted in writing
- Text messages or emails that contain all essential terms and show mutual assent

**What counts as "oral":**
- A verbal agreement with a handshake
- A written agreement where the disputed term was agreed to verbally
- An implied contract based on course of dealing (arguable — could be classified either way)

> **Practical takeaway:** If you're owed money on a verbal agreement, you have half the time to act. This is why we always recommend confirming important agreements in writing — even a simple email saying "To confirm our conversation: you agreed to pay $X by [date] for [service]" can transform an oral contract into a written one and double your enforcement window.

## What Happens If You Miss the Deadline?

Be blunt: your case is dead.

The defendant's attorney will file a demurrer or motion for summary judgment citing the expired statute of limitations. The court will grant it. Your claim is dismissed with prejudice — meaning you can never bring it again.

**Exceptions are extremely narrow:**
- You can argue the discovery rule delayed accrual
- You can argue tolling applied
- You can argue estoppel (the defendant's conduct prevented you from filing)
- You may have a separate claim with a longer statute (e.g., fraud instead of contract)

But these are arguments you make while hiring an attorney to fight a dismissal motion — they're not reliable strategies. The reliable strategy is to know your deadline and act months before it arrives.

## Special Rules for Government Claims

Suing a government entity in California (a city, county, the state, a public school district, CalTrans, etc.) has its own, much shorter timeline under the Government Claims Act (Gov. Code § 900 et seq.):

**You must file a government tort claim within 6 months of the incident** (Gov. Code § 911.2). This is not a lawsuit — it's a prerequisite administrative claim filed with the entity.

If the claim is rejected (or deemed rejected after 45 days of silence), you then have 6 months from the date of rejection to file the actual lawsuit (Gov. Code § 945.6).

Miss the 6-month administrative claim deadline? You can apply for late-claim relief within one year of the incident (Gov. Code § 911.4), but the entity can deny it, and courts have discretion.

**Bottom line:** If your dispute involves any government entity, you effectively have 6 months — not the standard 2-4 years.
