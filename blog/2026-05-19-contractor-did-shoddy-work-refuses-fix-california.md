# My Contractor Did Shoddy Work and Refuses to Fix It — What Can I Do in California?

If a California contractor did poor work and won't fix it, you can file a complaint with the Contractors State License Board (CSLB), send a formal demand letter, or sue in small claims court for up to $12,500. California Business & Professions Code §7106 makes substandard workmanship a cause for license discipline, which gives you real leverage.

That short answer matters because most homeowners who land here are staring at a botched kitchen remodel or a leaking roof the contractor swore was done right. The good news: California regulates contractors more aggressively than most states, and the tools available to you are practical, not just theoretical.

## What Counts as "Shoddy Work" Under California Law?

California doesn't use the phrase "shoddy work" in its statutes, but it doesn't need to. Business & Professions Code §7109 defines willful departure from accepted trade standards as a cause for disciplinary action. Civil Code §896 lists specific residential construction standards — everything from plumbing and electrical to waterproofing and structural integrity. If your contractor's work fails to meet the applicable building code or the specifications in your contract, you have a valid claim.

The practical test is this: would a competent, licensed contractor in the same trade have done the work this way? If the answer is no, you have grounds to act.

## Should You File a Complaint with the CSLB?

Yes — and you should do it early. The Contractors State License Board investigates complaints against licensed contractors at no cost to you. Under B&P Code §7011, the CSLB can order restitution, suspend or revoke licenses, and refer cases for criminal prosecution.

Filing a CSLB complaint also creates an official record. If you later need to go to court, having the CSLB investigation on file strengthens your position considerably. You can file online at the CSLB website, and the process typically takes 60–90 days for an initial response.

One important limitation: the CSLB can only act against licensed contractors. If your contractor was unlicensed, skip straight to the demand letter and small claims options below — and know that under B&P Code §7031, an unlicensed contractor may be required to return all compensation you paid.

## How Does a Demand Letter Help in a Contractor Dispute?

A [demand letter](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices-california) is a formal written notice telling the contractor exactly what went wrong, what you want done about it, and what happens if they don't respond. It's not a lawsuit — it's the step before a lawsuit that frequently makes the lawsuit unnecessary.

Most contractors respond to demand letters because ignoring one creates evidence of bad faith that a judge will see later. A well-drafted demand letter citing the specific contract provisions and code sections the contractor violated puts them on notice that you understand your rights and are prepared to enforce them.

## What About the Statute of Limitations?

Don't wait too long. California Code of Civil Procedure §337 gives you four years to sue on a written contract and §339 gives you two years for an oral one. For construction defect claims on new residential construction, Civil Code §895 et seq. sets different deadlines depending on the type of defect — some as short as one year for certain fit-and-finish issues, up to ten years for structural defects.

The clock usually starts when you discover the defect (or reasonably should have discovered it), not when the work was completed. But the longer you wait, the harder it becomes to prove causation and the easier it is for the contractor to argue the damage came from something else.

## What If the Contractor Is Unlicensed?

This actually works in your favor. Under B&P Code §7031(b), a person who uses the services of an unlicensed contractor may bring an action to recover all compensation paid. That's not just damages — it's everything you paid, regardless of whether some of the work was acceptable.

California courts take unlicensed contracting seriously. B&P Code §7028 makes it a misdemeanor to contract without a license, punishable by fines and potential jail time. This gives you additional leverage in any negotiation.

## What Steps Should You Take Right Now?

Document everything before you do anything else. Take photos and videos of the defective work. Gather your contract, all receipts, text messages, and emails with the contractor. Get a written assessment from another licensed contractor describing what was done wrong and what it will cost to fix.
