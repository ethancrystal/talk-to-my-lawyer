# LegalZoom vs. an Attorney-Signed Demand Letter: What's the Real Difference in California?

**Short answer:** The core difference is who stands behind the letter. A template or self-service product gives you a do-it-yourself document with your name on it; an attorney-signed demand letter is drafted and sent by a licensed California attorney on law-firm letterhead. The second carries far more weight with the recipient because it signals a lawyer is now involved.

Both can produce a letter. Only one tells the other side that legal action is a realistic next step — and that difference is usually what gets a stalled dispute moving.

## What do template services actually give you?

General-purpose legal sites are excellent for standardized paperwork — forming an LLC, a basic will, a simple template. When it comes to a demand letter, what you typically get is a fill-in-the-blanks document or a generic form you complete yourself. Your name goes at the bottom. There may be no California attorney reviewing your specific facts, and the letter arrives looking like something the claimant wrote.

That's fine for documenting a position. It's much weaker as *leverage*, because the recipient knows a self-written letter doesn't necessarily mean a lawyer is on the case.

## What does an attorney-signed letter give you?

An attorney-signed demand letter means a licensed California attorney reviews your situation, drafts the letter to fit your facts and the applicable law, and sends it on firm letterhead under their own signature. To the person on the other end, that letterhead changes the calculation: ignoring it now risks dealing with an actual lawyer, and potentially small claims or civil court.

This is why attorney-signed letters tend to get faster responses. The cost of ignoring them is higher.

## Does the signature really change the outcome?

In practice, yes — often dramatically. People who delete your emails and dodge your calls frequently pay within days of receiving a letter with an attorney's name on it. Nothing is guaranteed, and no honest provider will promise you'll win. But the perceived seriousness of an attorney-signed demand is a major part of what makes demand letters work at all. For more on what to expect after one goes out, see [what happens when you send a demand letter from a lawyer in California](/blog/2026-05-26-what-happens-when-you-send-a-demand-letter-from-a-lawyer-california).

## How do the costs compare?

A template letter is cheap or even free, but you're doing the work and carrying the risk that it lands without force. An attorney-signed flat-fee demand letter in California commonly runs around $199 — still far below hiring a lawyer hourly, while giving you the leverage of an actual attorney. For the broader pricing picture, see [how much a demand letter costs in California](/blog/2026-05-26-how-much-does-demand-letter-cost-california-2026).

The honest comparison isn't just price — it's price *relative to results*. A free letter that gets ignored costs you the debt; a $199 letter that gets you paid is the better value.

## When is a template good enough?

A self-service template can make sense when:

- The amount is **very small** and you mainly want a paper trail.
- The other side is **cooperative** and just needs a formal written request.
- You're comfortable the dispute won't escalate.

If you need real pressure — or the other side has been ignoring you — the attorney-signed route is built for exactly that.

## The bottom line

A template service gives you a document; an attorney-signed letter gives you leverage. In California, if your goal is to actually get paid or get someone to stop, the attorney signature is usually worth the modest flat fee. If you just need simple paperwork, a template may do.
