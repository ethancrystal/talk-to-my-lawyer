# Certified Mail, Email, or Text: How Should You Send a Demand Letter in California?

**Short answer: Certified mail with return receipt requested (USPS Form 3811) is the gold standard. It creates proof of delivery that California courts accept without argument. Email is a useful supplement — send both when possible. Text messages are risky: they lack formality, create chain-of-custody problems, and signal you're not serious enough to stamp an envelope.**

The question isn't just about getting the letter there. It's about proving it arrived — because if you end up in court, the judge will ask "did the defendant receive adequate notice?" and your answer needs to be airtight.

## Why Certified Mail Wins

California Code of Civil Procedure § 1013 establishes that mailing by registered or certified mail creates a presumption of receipt. The green return receipt card (PS Form 3811) is physical evidence signed by the recipient (or their agent) confirming delivery.


In small claims court, a judge seeing a signed return receipt card doesn't need testimony about delivery — the card speaks for itself. This matters because the standard question in any case where a demand letter was sent is: "Can you prove the other party received it before you filed suit?"

> **California CCP § 1013(a)** — Service by mail is complete upon deposit in the mail, but the recipient's time to respond is extended by 5 days (within California), 10 days (from out of state), or 20 days (from outside the US). Certified mail provides the additional evidence that it actually arrived.

## When Email Is Acceptable

Email works as a supplement — not a replacement. Send the demand letter via certified mail AND email on the same day. The email gets it there instantly (useful if the deadline is tight), and the certified copy provides the iron-clad proof.

Email is sufficient as the primary delivery method in two situations: (1) when the contract between you specifically designates email as the notice method, or (2) when prior communications were exclusively by email and you have no physical address.

The problem with email-only: recipients can claim it went to spam, that the attachment was corrupted, or that they never check that inbox. A "read receipt" email header is easy to block and doesn't constitute proof of delivery under California law.

## Why Text Messages Are a Bad Idea

A demand letter delivered by text message has three problems:

First, it signals you're not serious. A text message says "this might be a bluff." A letter on attorney letterhead that arrives by certified mail says "the next envelope will be a court summons."

Second, text messages create completeness problems. If the letter is longer than a few sentences (and it should be), it's going as an attachment or a link — neither of which guarantees the recipient read the full document.

Third, text messages are harder to authenticate in court. You'd need to prove the phone number belongs to the recipient, that they received it, and that the content wasn't altered in transmission.

## The Best Practice: Belt and Suspenders

Send the letter via certified mail AND email simultaneously. The email copy gets them reading immediately. The certified mail copy arrives 2-4 days later as the formal, provable delivery. Your deadline starts from the certified mail receipt date (giving them the full time you promised), and you've maximized the chance they actually read it.
