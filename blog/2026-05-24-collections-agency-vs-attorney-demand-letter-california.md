# Collections Agency vs. Attorney Demand Letter: Which Recovers More of Your Money in California?

A customer or business partner owes you money. You've followed up. Nothing. Now you're weighing two grown-up options: hire a collections agency, or pay a lawyer to send a demand letter. They look like the same product. They're not.

**Short answer:** Collections agencies typically keep 20–50% of what they recover and rely on volume and persistence. An attorney demand letter is a flat fee, you keep 100% of the recovery, and a single letter often resolves unpaid B2B invoices, broken contracts, and overdue commercial debts under about $25,000. For most California small businesses with a documented debt, the attorney letter recovers more net dollars per case.

Here's how the two stack up.

## How do collections agencies actually charge — and what do they keep?

Most third-party collections agencies work on contingency. They get paid a percentage of what they recover, and they keep the rest. Typical commission structures in California:

- **Fresh debts (under 90 days old):** 20–35% commission
- **Aged debts (90–180 days):** 30–45% commission
- **Older or harder debts:** 40–50%+ commission
- **Debts that require litigation:** Up to 50% plus a share of legal costs

The model is built on volume. Agencies file your account into a queue with hundreds of others, and the same automated calls, letters, and credit-bureau reporting techniques get applied across the board. For consumer debt, this can work — the FDCPA-bounded persistence wears people down.

For B2B debt, the dynamics are different. A business debtor who's ignoring you is usually making a calculated choice, and a collections agency's standard pressure (collection letters, phone calls, eventual credit reporting) doesn't always change that calculation. Worse, agencies often won't take the case at all if the debt is small, the debtor is out of state, or the documentation is thin.

When they do recover, the commission comes off the top. On a $5,000 invoice at a 35% commission, you net $3,250 — assuming full recovery, which isn't always the case.

## How is an attorney demand letter different?

An [attorney demand letter](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices) costs a flat fee paid up front. Whatever you recover, you keep.

The leverage is also different. A collections agency contacting a B2B debtor signals that the creditor has given up on direct resolution. A lawyer's letter signals that the creditor is preparing the documentation needed to file suit. For a business that cares about its credit, vendor relationships, or reputation, the second signal is the one that changes behavior.

Concrete contrast on the same $5,000 invoice:

- **Collections agency** at 35%: best-case net = $3,250.
- **Attorney letter** at $199 flat fee: best-case net = $4,801, assuming the letter prompts payment.

If the letter doesn't work, you're still free to escalate — to small claims (up to $12,500 for individuals, $6,250 for businesses), to limited civil court, or to a full lawsuit. The attorney letter doesn't burn any options. A collections agency, once involved, often complicates further legal action because they may have already issued threats or made representations on your behalf.

## When is collections the better choice?

Collections still wins in specific situations:

- **High volume of small consumer debts.** If you run an ecommerce site with dozens of chargebacks or a service business with many small unpaid invoices, the per-account cost of a collections agency is lower than paying for individual letters.
- **Unverified or hard-to-document debts.** Agencies will accept aged, partial-documentation accounts that an attorney might decline.
- **You want zero upfront cost.** Agencies are pure contingency. If you literally cannot pay a flat fee, that matters.
- **Out-of-state or hard-to-locate debtors.** National collections networks have skip-tracing infrastructure that an individual attorney letter doesn't replicate.

For everything else — documented B2B debts, broken contracts, deposits that should have been returned, single-customer disputes over $1,000 — an attorney letter usually leaves you with more cash in pocket per case.

## The decision matrix: which one fits your situation?

Use this quick guide.

**Choose attorney demand letter when:**

- The debt is between $1,000 and $25,000
- You have a written contract, invoice, or signed estimate
- The debtor is a business that values its reputation or credit
- This is one or a small number of accounts, not dozens
- You'd rather pay once and keep the recovery

**Choose collections agency when:**

- You have many small accounts at once
- The debts are aged and partially documented
- You can't afford or won't pay an upfront fee
- The debtor is out of state or hard to reach

**Consider both, in order:** Many California businesses send an attorney letter first. If that doesn't resolve the matter, they then place the account with collections — typically after the four-year statute of limitations clock (Code of Civil Procedure § 337 for written contracts) is well in mind. The letter creates the documented record; collections handles the long tail.

## When a letter is the right tool

If you have a documented California business debt under about $25,000, a single attorney demand letter is usually the highest net-recovery first step. It's a fixed cost, the leverage is sharper than a collections call, and you keep every dollar that comes back.
