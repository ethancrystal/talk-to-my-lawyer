# I Hired an Unlicensed Contractor in California and the Job Went Wrong — Can I Get My Money Back?

**Short answer:** Possibly all of it. Under California Business & Professions Code § 7031, an unlicensed contractor generally cannot sue to be paid, and the person who hired them can sue to recover **every dollar paid** — even if some work was completed. The first step is usually a demand letter citing § 7031 and the licensing requirement.

California treats contractor licensing seriously, and the law is unusually favorable to homeowners who got burned by an unlicensed operator. You don't have to prove the work was defective to use the strongest remedy — you only have to show the contractor wasn't properly licensed for the work.

## When does California require a contractor's license?

Anyone who contracts to perform construction work on a project valued at **$500 or more** in combined labor and materials must hold a license from the Contractors State License Board (CSLB). The narrow "minor work" exemption (Business & Professions Code § 7048) only covers jobs under that threshold. Most real home projects — remodels, additions, roofing, electrical, plumbing — are far above $500 and require a license.

## What does Business & Professions Code § 7031 actually say?

Section 7031 has two powerful halves:

- **§ 7031(a):** An unlicensed contractor **cannot bring a lawsuit to collect compensation** for work that required a license. So if they're chasing you for a final payment, that claim is generally barred.
- **§ 7031(b):** A person who hired an unlicensed contractor can **recover all compensation paid** for the unlicensed work — a remedy courts have applied even when the contractor did real work and the homeowner suffered no defects.

In other words, hiring an unlicensed contractor can entitle you to disgorgement of the entire amount you paid, not just the cost to fix problems.

## How do I confirm the contractor was unlicensed?

Check the CSLB license lookup using the contractor's name or the number on their contract or invoice. Confirm the license was **valid and active during the dates of your project** and that it covered the **classification** of the work performed. A lapsed license, a suspended license, or a license in the wrong classification can all support a § 7031 claim. Save screenshots and your contract and payment records.

## What's the first step to recovering my money?

Start with a written demand letter. It should identify the work, state that the contractor was unlicensed (or improperly licensed) for it, cite Business & Professions Code §§ 7031 and 7048, and demand return of the amounts paid by a firm deadline. Many unlicensed contractors fold quickly when they realize the law bars their own claim and exposes them to full repayment. A [breach of contract demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) can be adapted for this purpose.

## What if the contractor ignores the demand?

If the amount is within California's small claims limit — **$12,500 for an individual** — small claims court is a fast, lawyer-free venue, and § 7031 is exactly the kind of clean legal rule that does well there. For larger amounts, a civil suit is the backstop. You can also file a complaint with the CSLB, which can investigate and discipline unlicensed activity. For related options when a contractor takes money and disappears, see [contractor took your money and didn't finish](/blog/2026-05-28-contractor-took-money-didnt-finish-job-california-legal-options).

## Are there limits I should know about?

Yes. Section 7031's reach has been refined by the courts, and outcomes can turn on the facts — whether a license existed at all, whether it lapsed briefly, and timing under the statute of limitations. That's why a short attorney review before you send the demand is worthwhile: it confirms you're invoking the right subsection and deadlines so your strongest remedy stays available.
