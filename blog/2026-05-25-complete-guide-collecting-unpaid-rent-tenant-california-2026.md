## What California Law Actually Says

California landlord-tenant law sits primarily in the California Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) and Civil Code. The relevant framework for unpaid rent:

**CCP § 1161** defines unlawful detainer — the legal cause of action that gives landlords the right to reclaim possession. Nonpayment of rent after a proper 3-day notice is one of the grounds. This is the statute you'll hear about when people say "3-day notice."

**CCP § 116.220** sets small claims court limits. California small claims handles disputes up to $12,500. If your tenant owes less than that and has moved out, small claims may be faster than the eviction route for recovering the money.

**Civil Code § 1962** addresses lease terms related to rent payment — where, when, and how rent must be paid. Your lease should already specify this, but the statute fills gaps.

**CCP § 1174** covers the judgment a landlord can receive in an unlawful detainer action — not just possession, but the amount owed in back rent, court costs, and in some cases attorney fees if your lease has an attorney's fees clause.

## Option 1: The Demand Letter

This is the step most California landlords skip. Don't.

A formal attorney demand letter for unpaid rent is a document signed by a licensed California attorney that states the amount owed, the legal basis for the demand (your lease agreement, breach of contract), and a payment deadline — typically 10-14 days — with clear consequences if payment isn't received.

### Why it works

An attorney letter signals that the matter has moved past a landlord-tenant disagreement into actual legal territory. Your tenant isn't reading a frustrated landlord's email. They're reading something signed by an attorney who has presumably reviewed the case and is prepared to file.

For tenants who care about their rental history, their credit record, or avoiding a public court judgment, this is often enough. A voluntary payment — one the tenant makes because they've calculated that paying now is better than a judgment later — is the most reliable form of collection. Judgment enforcement in California is its own multi-year process.

### Who it works for

Demand letters work best when the tenant:
- Acknowledges owing some or all of the money
- Has some ability to pay (even partial)
- Cares about their rental and credit history
- Doesn't know how long the eviction process actually takes

They work less well when the tenant has already left with nothing, has no assets, or is experienced at gaming the unlawful detainer timeline.

## Option 2: The 3-Day Notice to Pay or Quit

Under CCP § 1161(2), before a California landlord can file for unlawful detainer, they must first serve the tenant with a 3-day notice to pay the overdue rent or vacate the premises. This is not optional — a properly served 3-day notice is a jurisdictional prerequisite.

### How to serve it

California law requires proper service. Personal delivery to the tenant is ideal. If the tenant isn't available, "substituted service" applies: you can leave it with someone of "suitable age and discretion" at the premises and mail a copy. As a last resort, you can post it on the front door and mail a copy (known as "nail and mail").

Keep a proof of service document — note the date, time, method, and who received the notice.

### What happens next

After 3 days: if the tenant pays in full, the 3-day notice is cured and no eviction can be filed for that nonpayment event. If the tenant doesn't pay and doesn't move out, you can file for unlawful detainer.

One important detail: accepting any partial payment after serving a 3-day notice may waive your right to file based on that notice. If you accept money after the notice is served, consult an attorney about your position.

## Option 3: Unlawful Detainer (Eviction)

Unlawful detainer is the formal eviction process in California. It gives you a court judgment — for possession of the unit and, usually, the back rent owed.

### The timeline

1. Serve 3-day notice. Wait 3 days.
2. File unlawful detainer complaint in superior court (county where the property is located). Filing fee: $200-$500 depending on rent amount.
3. Tenant is served by the sheriff or a registered process server. They have 5 calendar days to respond.
4. If they don't respond: you request a default judgment. Often quick.
5. If they do respond (contest): a hearing is scheduled, typically 20 days out — though some California counties run significantly longer.
6. Hearing. If you win: judgment for possession and back rent.
7. Writ of possession issued. Sheriff enforces it in 5-10 additional days.

**Total realistic timeline:** 45-90 days uncontested. 3-6 months or longer if contested in backlogged courts (Los Angeles, San Francisco, and some Bay Area counties are notoriously slow).

### The costs

Filing fees: $200-$500.

Attorney fees: $1,500-$4,000 to handle the full case if uncontested; $3,000-$7,000+ if contested.

If you file yourself (in propria persona), costs are lower — but unlawful detainer has procedural pitfalls. A defective notice, improper service, or procedural error can result in dismissal, and you'd have to start over.

### What you get

A judgment in your favor gives you: (1) possession of the unit, (2) a money judgment for the back rent, and (3) if your lease has an attorney's fees clause (which California law allows under Civil Code § 1717), potentially your legal fees.

What you don't automatically get: the money itself. A judgment is a paper right to collect. If your former tenant has no assets, no wages to garnish, no bank account you can reach, collecting the judgment may be difficult.

## Option 4: Small Claims Court

If the tenant has already vacated, small claims court at the county superior court is sometimes the faster path for recovering back rent and any damages to the property.

California small claims courts handle disputes up to $12,500 (CCP § 116.220). You can't use an attorney to represent you in small claims (though you can consult one beforehand). Filing fees run $30-$100.

Small claims cases are typically heard within 30-70 days of filing. If you win, you get a judgment. Collection — as with unlawful detainer judgments — depends on what the tenant has.

Small claims is not for recovering possession of the unit (only unlawful detainer handles that). It's for the money, after the tenant is gone.

## Choosing the Right Tool

| Situation | Best First Step |
|-----------|----------------|
| Tenant still there, first time non-payment | Attorney demand letter |
| Tenant still there, second month non-payment | Demand letter + 3-day notice |
| Tenant ignores everything, you want them out | Unlawful detainer |
| Tenant moved out, owes you money | Small claims court |
| Tenant moved out, owes more than $12,500 | Superior court lawsuit |
