If a neighbor is blocking your easement or right-of-way in California, you can usually force them to remove the obstruction through a court injunction — and most disputes resolve earlier with a demand letter. The key is identifying how your easement was created and proving it with records or a history of use.

Losing access to your own property is alarming, but California easement law is well developed and your rights, once documented, are enforceable.

## What is an easement, exactly?

An easement is a legal right to use someone else's land for a specific, limited purpose without owning it. The classic example is a right-of-way: the right to cross a neighbor's property to reach your own. Easements can also cover utilities, drainage, shared driveways, and parking.

California law (Civil Code section 801 and following) treats easements as real property interests. Two parcels are usually involved: the *dominant tenement*, which benefits, and the *servient tenement*, which bears the burden. An easement generally "runs with the land," meaning it stays attached to the property and passes to future owners. Knowing which type of easement you have is the first step in any dispute, because each is created and proven differently.

## How are easements created in California?

### Express easements

The clearest easements are created in writing — in a deed, a grant of easement, or a recorded agreement. An express easement spells out its location, width, and permitted uses. Because it is recorded, it offers the strongest evidence and the easiest path to enforcement. Always start by checking your deed, your title report, and the county recorder's records.

### Easements by implication

Sometimes an easement is implied from the circumstances when a larger parcel was divided. If, before the split, one part of the property was openly and continuously used to benefit another part — for example, a single driveway serving what became two lots — and that use is reasonably necessary, a court may find an implied easement based on the parties' presumed intent.

### Easements by necessity

When a parcel is landlocked, with no legal access to a public road except across neighboring land that was once under common ownership, California courts may recognize an easement by necessity. The core requirements are a prior common ownership and strict necessity for access. The doctrine exists because the law disfavors land that cannot be used at all.

### Prescriptive easements

A prescriptive easement arises from long-term use rather than agreement. To establish one in California, the user must show open and notorious, continuous, and adverse (without permission) use of a defined area for five years. Unlike adverse possession, a prescriptive easement does not require paying property taxes and does not transfer ownership — it only grants the right to continue the established use.

## What are the most common easement disputes?

Easement conflicts tend to follow a few patterns:

- **Blocked access.** The servient owner installs a gate, fence, wall, or landscaping that prevents you from using the right-of-way.
- **Overburdening.** The dominant owner expands the use beyond the easement's original scope — more traffic, a wider path, or commercial use of a residential right-of-way.
- **Maintenance disputes.** The parties disagree about who must repair and maintain a shared road or driveway.
- **Scope and location.** The owners disagree about exactly where the easement runs or what it allows.
- **Termination claims.** One party argues the easement was abandoned or is no longer necessary.

Each turns on the easement's origin and terms, which is why documentation is so important.

## What does the law require of each owner?

An easement creates duties on both sides. The servient owner (whose land is burdened) generally may not interfere with your reasonable use and may not obstruct the easement. At the same time, you (the dominant owner) must keep your use within the easement's scope and purpose and, absent an agreement otherwise, are typically responsible for maintaining the easement you use. Where multiple owners share a private road, California law addresses proportional maintenance among them.

## What remedies do I have if my easement is blocked?

If a neighbor obstructs your easement, California courts can provide several forms of relief:

- **Injunction.** A court can order the neighbor to remove the obstruction — a gate, fence, or landscaping — and to stop interfering. Injunctive relief is the most common remedy for blocked access, because money alone does not restore the use.
- **Damages.** You may recover for harm caused by the interference, such as the cost of alternative access or loss of use.
- **Quiet title or declaratory relief.** Where the existence, scope, or location of the easement is disputed, a court can declare the parties' rights definitively, clearing up the title for the future.

If you are the servient owner and believe the dominant owner is overburdening the easement, you can likewise ask a court to limit the use to its proper scope.

## How do I protect and enforce my access?

1. **Pull the records.** Obtain your deed, a current title report, and any recorded easement documents from the county recorder. These define your rights.
2. **Map the easement.** If the location is unclear, a licensed surveyor can plot the easement against the obstruction.
3. **Document the interference.** Photograph the gate, fence, or landscaping blocking access, and keep a log of dates and communications.
4. **Preserve evidence of long use.** If you rely on a prescriptive or implied easement, gather proof of how long and how openly the use has continued — witness statements and older photos help.
5. **Send a demand letter.** A formal letter setting out your easement and demanding removal of the obstruction is often the decisive step.
6. **Escalate if necessary.** If the neighbor refuses, an action for injunctive relief, damages, or quiet title may follow.

## Why does a demand letter work so well here?

Litigation over real property is expensive and slow, and many neighbors simply do not appreciate the strength of a recorded or established easement until it is laid out clearly. A professional demand letter identifies the easement and its source, describes the specific obstruction, cites the relevant California easement principles, and demands a concrete remedy — such as removing a gate or restoring access — by a reasonable deadline.

Because it is firm, specific, and well-supported, the letter shows you understand your rights and are prepared to enforce them, which is often enough to prompt compliance. It also builds your record: if the matter does proceed to court, your dated, detailed demand demonstrates that you asserted your rights and gave the neighbor a chance to cure. The same dynamic makes [demand letters](/blog/2026-05-13-what-is-a-demand-letter) effective across property disputes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can my neighbor put a gate across my right-of-way?
Not if it unreasonably interferes with your use. A servient owner generally cannot obstruct an easement. In some cases a gate may be allowed if it does not meaningfully impair access (for example, one you have a key to), but blocking the easement is not permitted.

### How long must I use a path to claim a prescriptive easement?
Five years of open, notorious, continuous, and adverse (non-permissive) use in California. Unlike adverse possession, you do not need to pay property taxes, and you gain a right to use the land, not ownership of it.

### What if my property is landlocked?
If your parcel has no legal access to a public road except over land once under common ownership with yours, you may have an easement by necessity, recognized so the land is not rendered unusable.

### Who pays to maintain a shared easement?
Absent a written agreement, the owner who uses the easement generally bears its maintenance. Where several owners share a private road or driveway, California law addresses proportionate shared responsibility based on use. A written maintenance agreement avoids most disputes.

### Can an easement be terminated or changed?
Yes, under certain conditions — a written release, abandonment shown by clear intent to give up the right, merger of the two properties under one owner, or expiration of a term easement. Mere non-use usually is not enough by itself. If your neighbor claims your easement no longer exists, ask them to identify the specific legal basis; in most cases a recorded easement remains fully enforceable until formally released.

### Does my easement transfer when I sell the property?
Generally, yes. An easement that benefits your land is "appurtenant" and runs with the property, passing automatically to the new owner when you sell — you do not have to re-create it. Likewise, the burden stays with the servient parcel even if that neighbor sells. This is why checking the recorded easement during a purchase matters: the rights and obligations you take on come with the land, not with the previous owner.

## How long do I have to act if my easement is blocked?

Do not wait indefinitely. While a recorded easement does not simply disappear, allowing an obstruction to remain unchallenged can create practical and legal complications. If the servient owner openly and continuously uses the easement area in a way inconsistent with your rights — for instance, treating a blocked section as exclusively their own — for a long enough period, they may eventually argue that your easement was abandoned or that they acquired countervailing rights. Prompt, written objection protects you. A dated demand letter interrupts any such argument by making clear that the obstruction is contested, not accepted. Acting early also tends to keep the dispute smaller and cheaper to resolve, before structures, landscaping, or expectations become entrenched.

Easement disputes strike at something fundamental — the ability to reach and use your property. The good news is that your rights, once identified and documented, are enforceable, and a precise demand letter often restores access without the cost of litigation.
