# They Responded to Your Demand Letter — How to Negotiate and Close the Settlement in California

**Short answer: When someone responds to your demand letter, respond within 48 hours, treat their reply as the opening of a negotiation (not a final offer), never accept less than your documented damages without a strategic reason, and always get the final agreement in writing — a signed settlement agreement that's enforceable under CCP § 664.6.**

You sent the demand letter. You waited. And now — they responded.

This is the moment most people fumble. They either accept a lowball offer out of relief that someone responded at all, or they get aggressive and blow up a deal that was already 80% done.

Neither approach gets you paid. Here's the playbook that does.

## What Does Their Response Actually Mean?

Before you respond to their response, classify what you received. Demand letter responses fall into four categories:

**Category 1: Full acceptance.** They agree to pay the full amount by the deadline. This is rare (roughly 15-20% of responses) but it happens, especially with documented debts and clear liability. Skip to the settlement agreement section below.

**Category 2: Partial acceptance with counteroffer.** They acknowledge they owe something, dispute the amount, and propose a lower figure. This is the most common response (40-50%) and the best negotiating position for you — they've conceded liability.

**Category 3: Denial with explanation.** They dispute the claim entirely but explain why. This tells you their defense theory, which is valuable intelligence for your next move.

**Category 4: Denial with threat.** They deny everything and threaten counterclaims, attorney fees, or defamation suits. In California, this is often bluster (Civil Code § 47 protects good-faith pre-litigation communications). Don't be intimidated.

## The 48-Hour Rule: Why Response Speed Matters

When someone responds to a demand letter, they're in a brief window of engagement. They've consulted with someone (an attorney, a business partner, their spouse), decided this requires attention, and drafted a reply. That attention is perishable.

**Respond within 48 hours.** Not because the law requires it, but because:
- Their motivation to resolve this is at its peak
- Delay signals you're not serious or that the matter isn't urgent to you
- They may interpret silence as acceptance of their counteroffer
- Other priorities will push your dispute down their list

**What your response should contain:**
1. Acknowledgment that you received their reply
2. Your specific reaction to each point they raised
3. A clear counter-proposal (if applicable)
4. A new deadline — typically 7-10 days
5. A statement of what happens if no agreement is reached (e.g., "We will proceed with filing in Superior Court")

## How to Negotiate Without Losing the Deal

The fundamental rule of demand letter negotiation in California: **the person willing to walk away has the leverage, but the person who makes it easy to say yes gets paid.**

**Negotiation framework:**

**Know your BATNA.** Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement. What happens if negotiation fails? If your BATNA is small claims court (fast, cheap, $12,500 max), you have different leverage than if it's unlimited civil (expensive, slow, full recovery possible).

**Anchor high, but within reason.** Your demand letter amount was your anchor. If they counter at 50%, don't immediately split the difference. Counter at 85-90% of your original demand and explain why.

**Concede strategically.** Give on terms that cost you little but feel like wins for them:
- Extended payment timeline (you get the same amount, just slower)
- Remove a non-monetary demand (an apology, a public statement)
- Reduce interest calculation
- Waive late fees while keeping the principal intact

**Never concede liability language.** If they want "without admission of liability" in the settlement, that's fine — standard boilerplate. But never agree to language that says you were wrong to make the demand, that the debt didn't exist, or that you'll pay their attorney fees for having to respond.

| Their Move | Your Counter | Why It Works |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| "We'll pay 50%" | "I'll accept 85% if paid within 14 days" | Gives urgency incentive without collapsing your number |
| "We need 90 days" | "60 days with 10% payment now as good faith" | Partial immediate payment creates commitment |
| "We dispute the amount" | "Here's the documentation — let's discuss which items you contest" | Forces specificity instead of vague denial |
| "Our attorney says we owe nothing" | "I'll proceed with filing unless we resolve by [date]" | Calls the bluff without hostility |
| "We can only afford $X" | "Show me documentation of inability to pay the full amount" | Tests whether it's a negotiating tactic or reality |

## The Three Settlement Structures That Actually Work

Not every settlement is a lump-sum check. California law supports several structures:

**1. Lump-sum payment.** Full amount within a specified deadline (typically 14-30 days). Best when the amount is under $10,000 and the debtor has the funds.

**2. Installment plan.** Monthly payments over 3-12 months. Include an acceleration clause: if they miss one payment, the entire remaining balance becomes due immediately. Under CCP § 85.1, you can enforce a stipulated judgment that kicks in automatically on default.

**3. Partial payment plus non-monetary consideration.** They pay 70% in cash and provide something else of value — a service credit, return of property, a favorable reference, termination of a contract without penalty. Creative settlements often get both sides closer to yes.

> **Statute callout:** CCP § 664.6 allows parties to request the court to enter judgment per the terms of a signed settlement agreement. This is your enforcement mechanism. If they sign an agreement and then don't pay, you can get a court judgment without a full trial — just a motion proving the agreement exists and was breached.

## Writing the Settlement Agreement: Non-Negotiable Terms

A handshake or email saying "fine, I'll pay" is not a settlement. You need a written agreement that's enforceable in California courts. Every settlement agreement should include these elements:

**Identification of the parties** — full legal names (not just "John" — "John Michael Smith, an individual residing in Los Angeles County, California").

**Recital of the dispute** — briefly describe what the dispute was about. This establishes consideration and context.

**Payment terms** — exact amount, payment method, deadline. For installments: each payment date, the monthly amount, and where to send it.

**Acceleration clause** (for installments) — "In the event of default on any payment, the entire remaining balance becomes immediately due and payable."

**Release of claims** — what claims each side is releasing. Be specific: "Claimant releases all claims arising from [the specific transaction/event]." Don't release future claims or unrelated claims.

**Confidentiality** (optional) — whether either party can discuss the settlement terms. Common in business disputes.

**CCP § 664.6 stipulation** — "The parties agree that in the event of a breach of this agreement, either party may move the court for entry of judgment pursuant to CCP § 664.6."

**Integration clause** — "This agreement constitutes the entire agreement between the parties and supersedes all prior negotiations and agreements."

**Signatures and date** — both parties sign. Electronic signatures are valid in California under the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (Civil Code § 1633.1 et seq.).

## Common Mistakes That Kill Settlements

**Mistake 1: Accepting a verbal agreement.** "I'll send you a check next week" is not enforceable. Get it in writing before you stop pursuing the claim.

**Mistake 2: Negotiating against yourself.** If they countered at $5,000 on a $10,000 claim, don't immediately drop to $6,000. Let them justify their number first.

**Mistake 3: Threatening litigation you won't follow through on.** If you say "I'll file in Superior Court by Friday" and then don't, your credibility evaporates. Only set deadlines you'll enforce.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring the statute of limitations.** While you're negotiating, the clock is still ticking. If your limitations period is close to expiring (check our guide on [California statutes of limitations](/blog/how-long-to-sue-california-statutes-of-limitations-2026)), file a protective complaint while continuing to negotiate. Under CCP § 583.210, you have three years after filing to serve the defendant — but filing preserves your rights.

**Mistake 5: Releasing too broadly.** A release that says "all claims of any kind, known or unknown" could waive claims you didn't even know about. California Civil Code § 1542 specifically addresses this — any waiver of unknown claims must explicitly reference § 1542, or it may not be enforceable.

## What If They Stop Responding Mid-Negotiation?

It happens. You exchange two productive emails, then silence. Here's the protocol:

1. **Day 3 after last contact:** Send a brief follow-up. "Checking in on our conversation from [date]. Please let me know your position by [new date]."

2. **Day 7:** Send a final demand reiterating your last offer with a hard deadline. "This offer remains open until [date]. After that, I will proceed with [specific next step]."

3. **After the deadline passes:** Follow through. File in small claims, limited civil, or unlimited civil depending on the amount. Don't send a fourth letter.

The silence itself is useful information. It usually means one of three things: they consulted an attorney who told them not to communicate further, they're stalling to avoid payment, or life got in the way. Your filing will clarify which one.
