# What Is a Notice of Intent to Lien and Does It Actually Work in California?

**Short answer:** A Notice of Intent to Lien is a formal written warning that you will record a mechanics lien against a property unless you're paid by a stated deadline. In California it is not legally required before most liens, but it works because it shows you know your rights and are ready to cloud the owner's title — which pressures payment fast.

If you're a contractor, subcontractor, or supplier who hasn't been paid, the Notice of Intent (sometimes called a "Notice of Intent to Lien" or "pre-lien demand") sits between a polite invoice reminder and the nuclear option of recording an actual lien. It tells the recipient exactly what happens next if they keep ignoring you.

## How is a Notice of Intent different from a mechanics lien?

A **mechanics lien** is recorded with the county and attaches to the property's title — it can block a sale or refinance until resolved. A **Notice of Intent to Lien** is just a letter you send first. Nothing is recorded. It says: "I am owed $X for work performed at [address]. If I am not paid within [usually 10 days], I will record a mechanics lien under California Civil Code § 8400 and pursue all remedies available."

Think of it as the last off-ramp before things hit the public record.

## Is a Notice of Intent required in California?

For a standard private-works mechanics lien, no — California does not require a Notice of Intent before recording (unlike a few other states). What California **does** require is the **20-day preliminary notice** (Civil Code § 8200) for most subcontractors and suppliers to preserve lien rights in the first place. Don't confuse the two: the preliminary notice protects your right to lien; the Notice of Intent is a strategic warning that you're about to use it.

Sending the Notice of Intent anyway is smart because it creates a paper trail, gives the owner a clear deadline, and often resolves the debt before you incur recording costs.

## Why does a Notice of Intent get results?

Owners and general contractors take title clouds seriously. A recorded lien can:

- Stall a pending home sale or refinance.
- Trigger penalty exposure if they wrongfully refuse to release it later.
- Force them into a foreclosure lawsuit timeline (the claimant must sue within 90 days of recording under Civil Code § 8460).

When a contractor signals they understand all of this, many owners pay rather than let the lien hit the record. The Notice of Intent converts a vague "we'll get to it" into a concrete decision with a deadline.

## What should a California Notice of Intent include?

- Your name and role (contractor, sub, or supplier).
- The property address and owner/GC name.
- The amount owed and what it covers.
- A reference to your preliminary notice, if you served one.
- A firm payment deadline (commonly 10 days).
- A clear statement that you'll record a mechanics lien and pursue collection if unpaid.

Pair it with the underlying contract and invoices so the demand is documented and hard to dispute. If the work is also the subject of a payment dispute, a [breach of contract demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) can be combined with the lien warning.

## Does an attorney-signed Notice of Intent work better?

Often, yes. A letter on law-firm letterhead signals that the next step isn't an empty threat. It also reduces the chance you misstate a deadline or waive a right by accident — mechanics lien law is unforgiving about timing. For many California contractors, a single attorney-drafted Notice of Intent costs a fraction of recording and litigating a lien, and it resolves the debt before deadlines become a problem. See how this compares to filing a [mechanics lien directly](/blog/2026-06-02-mechanics-lien-vs-demand-letter-california-contractor).

## What if the deadline passes and I still haven't been paid?

Then you move forward: record the mechanics lien within your statutory window (generally 90 days after completion for a direct contractor, or 60 days after a recorded Notice of Completion), and be ready to file a foreclosure action within 90 days of recording. Missing either deadline can wipe out your lien rights — which is exactly why the Notice of Intent, sent early, is so valuable.
