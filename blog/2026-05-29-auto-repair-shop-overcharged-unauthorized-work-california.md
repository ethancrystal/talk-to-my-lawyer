# An Auto Repair Shop Overcharged Me or Did Unauthorized Work — How Do I Fight It in California?

**Short answer:** In California, an auto repair shop must give you a written estimate and **cannot charge more than 10% over it** without your specific authorization. It also can't perform repairs you never approved. If a shop broke these rules, you can dispute the bill, file a complaint with the Bureau of Automotive Repair, and demand a refund of the overcharge.

California regulates auto repair tightly precisely because overcharging and surprise work are common complaints. The law gives you concrete leverage.

## What does California law require of repair shops?

The **Automotive Repair Act**, California Business & Professions Code § 9884 and following, sets the rules every registered shop must follow:

- **Written estimate first.** The shop must give you a written estimate for parts and labor before starting work.
- **No charge above the estimate without consent.** The shop **cannot charge more than the estimated price** unless it gets your authorization for the additional work — and the law treats a charge of more than 10% over the estimate as the kind of overage that requires your approval.
- **No unauthorized work.** The shop can't do repairs you never agreed to and then bill you for them.
- **Return of parts.** On request, the shop must return your replaced parts.

A shop that violates these rules has broken the law, and that's the foundation of your dispute.

## What counts as unauthorized work?

Anything beyond what you approved in writing or by documented verbal consent. If you authorized a brake job and the invoice also bills you for a fuel-system flush you never okayed, that flush is unauthorized. You generally don't have to pay for work you didn't approve.

## How do I dispute the bill?

1. **Get the paperwork.** Collect your written estimate, the final invoice, and any texts or emails about the work.
2. **Compare line by line.** Identify charges that exceed the estimate or cover work you didn't authorize.
3. **Raise it with the shop in writing.** Ask for a corrected invoice or a refund of the overcharge, citing the Automotive Repair Act, by a set deadline.
4. **File a BAR complaint.** The California **Bureau of Automotive Repair** investigates these complaints and can mediate. Shops take BAR complaints seriously because BAR licenses them.
5. **Dispute the charge** with your card issuer if you paid by card and are still in the dispute window.

## Can I refuse to pay or get my car back?

This gets tricky. A shop may assert a **mechanic's lien** to hold your vehicle until the bill is paid. Often the practical move is to pay under protest (note "paid under protest" on the payment), retrieve your car, and then pursue the refund through a BAR complaint, a demand letter, or small claims — rather than leaving the vehicle.

## When should I send a demand letter?

If the shop won't correct an obvious overcharge or unauthorized charge, a formal demand letter that cites § 9884 and references your BAR complaint often gets a refund without a lawsuit. For amounts the shop still refuses to return, California small claims court handles disputes up to $12,500.
