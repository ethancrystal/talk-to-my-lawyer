# A Contractor Filed a Mechanics Lien on My House in California — What Can I Do?

**Short answer:** You can dispute a mechanics lien by demanding the contractor record a release, especially if the lien is inflated, late, or filed by someone you already paid. In California, a lien expires if the claimant doesn't sue to foreclose within 90 days of recording (Civil Code § 8460). A demand letter often gets a wrongful lien removed without court.

A mechanics lien is a legal claim recorded against your property's title by a contractor, subcontractor, or material supplier who says they weren't paid for work on your home. It is serious — an unresolved lien can block a sale or refinance — but it is not the same as owing the money. Plenty of California liens are defective, overstated, or filed after the legal deadline, and those can be cleared.

## Is the lien even valid?

California's mechanics lien law (Civil Code § 8400 and following) has strict rules, and a missed step makes a lien unenforceable. Check these first:

- **Preliminary notice.** Most subcontractors and suppliers must serve a "20-day preliminary notice" to preserve lien rights (Civil Code § 8200). A sub who never served one usually can't lien.
- **Recording deadline.** A direct contractor generally must record within 90 days after the project is complete, or 60 days after the owner records a Notice of Completion (Civil Code § 8412–8414). A late lien is invalid.
- **Foreclosure deadline.** After recording, the claimant has only 90 days to file a lawsuit to enforce the lien (Civil Code § 8460). If they don't sue in that window, the lien becomes unenforceable and you can demand its removal.
- **Amount.** A lien can only cover the reasonable value of labor and materials actually provided — not penalties, not padded extras.

## What if I already paid the general contractor?

This is the most painful scenario: you paid your general contractor in full, but the GC never paid a subcontractor, and now the sub has liened your home. Under California's "double payment" risk, the sub may still have a valid lien against your property even though you did nothing wrong.

Your protection is the **conditional and unconditional lien waiver** (Civil Code § 8132–8138). Going forward, never release a payment without collecting a signed waiver from every sub and supplier. For a lien that's already filed, a demand letter to both the GC and the sub — pointing out the double payment and demanding a release — frequently resolves it, because the sub's real dispute is with the GC, not you.

## How do I get a wrongful lien removed?

Start with a written demand that the claimant record a "Release of Mechanics Lien." California law actually penalizes a claimant who refuses to release a lien they're no longer entitled to: once the lien is unenforceable or paid, they must release it within 10 days of a written request, or face liability for damages and attorney's fees (Civil Code § 8480 and § 8482).

A demand letter from an attorney carries weight here because it signals you know the deadlines and the penalty statute. If that fails, you can file a **petition to release the lien** in superior court (Civil Code § 8480), and a prevailing owner can recover attorney's fees — which gives the claimant a strong reason to settle first.

A [breach of contract demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) can also be paired with the release demand when the contractor both did poor work and filed an aggressive lien.

## Should I just pay it to make it go away?

Not before you check validity. If the lien is late, lacks a preliminary notice, or the 90-day foreclosure window has passed, you may owe nothing. If the underlying work really was unpaid and properly liened, paying or negotiating a reduced payoff in exchange for a recorded release may be the cleanest path. The point is to decide from a position of knowledge — not panic over a title cloud.

## When does a lawyer's letter make sense?

If the lien is blocking a sale or refinance, was filed by a sub you already paid through, or is plainly past deadline, an attorney-signed letter citing the specific Civil Code sections and the 10-day release requirement is often all it takes. It costs far less than litigation and moves faster than a court petition.
