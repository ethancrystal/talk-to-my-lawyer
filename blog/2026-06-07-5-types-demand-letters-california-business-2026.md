# 5 Types of Demand Letters Every California Business Should Know (2026)

**Short answer: The five demand letter types California businesses use most are: (1) payment demands for unpaid invoices, (2) cease-and-desist letters for IP or behavior violations, (3) breach of contract notices, (4) security deposit demand letters, and (5) preliminary notice / intent-to-lien letters for construction. Each serves a different legal function and triggers different statutory protections.**

Most people think "demand letter" is one thing. It isn't. The phrase covers at least five distinct legal instruments, each with its own purpose, required elements, and California statutory framework. Using the wrong type — or omitting required elements from the right type — can waste your money or waive your rights.

Here's each type, when to use it, and what California law requires.

## Type 1: Payment Demand Letter (Unpaid Invoice / Debt Collection)

**Purpose:** Collect money owed under a contract, invoice, or agreement.

**When to send:** After informal reminders fail. Typically sent 30-60 days after the payment was due.

**Required elements under California law:**
- Specific amount owed (down to the cent)
- Basis for the debt (contract date, invoice number, description of work)
- Deadline to pay (standard: 15-30 days)
- Statement of consequences (small claims filing, interest accrual, credit reporting)

**Key statute:** CCP § 337 gives you 4 years to sue on a written contract. But under Commercial Code § 2725, sales of goods have a shorter 4-year window from tender of delivery.

**Special California rule for freelancers:** Under SB 988 (Labor Code § 2810.5 et seq.), clients who fail to pay freelancers for completed work within 30 days of the agreed date are liable for double damages. Your demand letter should cite this statute — it turns a $3,000 claim into a $6,000 exposure.


> **California Labor Code § 2810.5 (SB 988)** — Freelance workers who are not paid by the contracted date may recover double the unpaid amount in a civil action. A demand letter citing this statute tells the client: settle at face value now, or face 2x liability in court.

## Type 2: Cease-and-Desist Letter (IP Infringement / Harmful Behavior)

**Purpose:** Stop someone from doing something — using your trademark, copying your content, defaming your business, violating a non-compete.

**When to send:** As soon as you discover the infringing or harmful activity. Delay can weaken your claim (laches defense).

**Required elements:**
- Specific description of the infringing activity
- Your ownership rights (trademark registration number, copyright date, contract clause)
- Demand to stop immediately
- Deadline to confirm cessation (typically 10-14 days)
- Consequences of non-compliance (DMCA takedown, trademark infringement suit, injunction)

**Key statutes:**
- Trademark: Lanham Act § 43(a) (federal) + Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 14245 (state)
- Copyright: 17 U.S.C. § 504 (statutory damages up to $150,000 per willful infringement)
- Trade secrets: Cal. Civ. Code § 3426 (CUTSA)

**When it's legally required:** Under the DMCA (17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3)), you must send a proper takedown notice before a platform is obligated to remove infringing content. For trademark disputes on platforms like Amazon, a cease-and-desist to the seller is often required before the platform acts.

## Type 3: Breach of Contract Notice

**Purpose:** Formally notify the other party they've breached a contract and give them an opportunity to cure (fix the breach) before you pursue remedies.

**When to send:** After the breach occurs and informal communication hasn't resolved it.

**Required elements:**
- Identification of the contract (date, parties, subject)
- Specific clause(s) breached
- How the breach occurred (facts, dates, evidence)
- Cure period (if the contract specifies one — many do)
- Damages sought or remedy demanded
- Statement of intent to pursue legal remedies if not cured

**Critical California requirement:** Many contracts include a "notice and cure" provision requiring you to give written notice and a specified cure period (often 10-30 days) before declaring a breach. If you skip this and go straight to court, the defendant can argue you failed to follow the contract's own dispute resolution procedure — potentially getting your case dismissed.

**Key statute:** Cal. Civ. Code § 1511 — When a contract requires notice of breach, failure to provide proper notice can excuse the other party's non-performance.

## Type 4: Security Deposit Demand Letter

**Purpose:** Demand return of a security deposit from a landlord who has failed to return it within California's statutory deadline.

**When to send:** 21+ days after move-out. Under Civ. Code § 1950.5(g), landlords must return the deposit (or provide an itemized statement of deductions) within 21 calendar days of the tenant surrendering the unit.

**Required elements:**
- Move-out date and unit address
- Amount of deposit paid (with proof — cancelled check, lease showing amount)
- Statement that 21 days have passed without return or proper itemization
- Demand for full return within a specified period (typically 7-14 days)
- Citation to Civ. Code § 1950.5
- Statement of intent to file in small claims if not returned

**Why this type has the highest success rate:** California law is extremely clear on security deposits. There's almost no factual dispute possible about the timeline (21 days is 21 days), and the penalty for landlord non-compliance is up to 2x the deposit amount in bad faith cases (Civ. Code § 1950.5(l)). Landlords who receive these letters know the law favors the tenant overwhelmingly.

> **California Civil Code § 1950.5(l)** — If a landlord retains a security deposit in bad faith, the tenant may recover up to twice the deposit amount plus actual damages. A demand letter citing this section tells the landlord exactly what they'll face in small claims court.

## Type 5: Preliminary Notice / Intent to Lien (Construction)

**Purpose:** Protect a contractor's or supplier's right to file a mechanics lien if they're not paid for work on a California construction project.

**When to send:** Within 20 days of first providing labor, services, or materials to the project. This is not technically a "demand" letter — it's a statutory prerequisite to lien rights. But it functions as a demand because it puts the property owner on notice that unpaid work creates a lien against their property.

**Required elements (per Civ. Code § 8034):**
- Description of labor/services/materials furnished
- Name of person who contracted for the work
- Name of the property owner (if known)
- Description of the job site (address)
- Statement that the sender may file a mechanics lien if not paid

**Key deadlines:**
- **20 days** from first furnishing: deadline to serve preliminary notice (Civ. Code § 8204)
- **90 days** after completion: deadline to record the mechanics lien (Civ. Code § 8412)
- **90 days** after recording: deadline to file suit to enforce the lien (Civ. Code § 8460)

**Why contractors should always send this:** Missing the 20-day preliminary notice deadline means losing mechanics lien rights entirely — and a mechanics lien is often the most powerful collection tool a contractor has. It attaches to the property itself, meaning the owner can't sell or refinance without resolving it.

## How to Choose the Right Type

The decision tree is simpler than it looks:

**Someone owes you money for goods/services delivered?** → Payment demand letter.

**Someone is using your intellectual property or doing something harmful?** → Cease-and-desist.

**Someone violated a specific contract term?** → Breach of contract notice. Check the contract for required cure periods first.

**Your landlord kept your security deposit past 21 days?** → Security deposit demand letter.

**You did construction work and haven't been paid?** → Preliminary notice (within 20 days of starting work) followed by a payment demand if still unpaid.
