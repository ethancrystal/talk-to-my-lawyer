# How California Contractors Can Collect a Final Payment a Client Refuses to Pay

**Short answer:** A California contractor can collect a withheld final payment by documenting the completed work, sending a written demand with a deadline, preserving mechanics lien rights, and invoking prompt-payment penalties (up to 2% per month under Business & Professions Code § 7108.5 in some contexts). Most disputes settle after a firm attorney demand — before any lien or lawsuit.

The job's done, the client moved in or reopened, and your final invoice is "under review" for the third week running. This is one of the most common — and most fixable — problems in California construction. The key is to act in the right order so you protect your deadlines while giving the client every reason to just pay.

## Step 1: Confirm the work is documented as complete

Before you escalate, gather proof that you performed: the signed contract or change orders, dated photos, inspection sign-offs, and your invoices. Note the **date of completion** — it starts the clock on your mechanics lien rights (generally 90 days for a direct contractor under Civil Code § 8414). If you're licensed, confirm your CSLB license was active during the work; an unlicensed contractor generally cannot sue to collect at all under Business & Professions Code § 7031.

## Step 2: Send a written payment demand with a deadline

A clear demand letter usually does more than another invoice. It should state the amount owed, reference the contract, attach proof of completion, and give a firm deadline (often 10 days). Mention that you're prepared to record a mechanics lien and pursue interest and remedies if unpaid.

California allows **10% annual prejudgment interest** on a liquidated contract debt (Civil Code § 3289), and written contracts carry a **four-year statute of limitations** (Code of Civil Procedure § 337) — so the client can't simply run out the clock. A [demand letter for unpaid invoices](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices) lays out the structure.

## Step 3: Preserve and, if needed, use your mechanics lien

If the deadline passes, a mechanics lien is your strongest leverage because it attaches to the property's title. To keep that option alive:

- Serve a **20-day preliminary notice** at the start of the job if you're a sub or supplier (Civil Code § 8200).
- Record the lien within your window — generally 90 days after completion, or 60 days after the owner records a Notice of Completion (Civil Code § 8412–8414).
- Be ready to file a foreclosure suit within 90 days of recording (Civil Code § 8460), or the lien lapses.

Even the credible threat of a lien, delivered in a [Notice of Intent to Lien](/blog/2026-06-02-what-is-a-notice-of-intent-to-lien-california), often unlocks payment.

## Step 4: Invoke prompt-payment rules where they apply

California's prompt-payment statutes can add penalties on top of the debt. For example, when a prime contractor wrongfully withholds money owed to a subcontractor, Business & Professions Code § 7108.5 can impose a 2%-per-month penalty plus attorney's fees. Public-works and private-works progress payments have their own prompt-payment timelines (Civil Code § 8800 and following). These penalties give the paying party a real cost for stalling — cite them when they apply.

## Step 5: Small claims or a lawsuit as the backstop

If the amount is within California's small claims limit — **$12,500 for an individual** (lower for business entities) — small claims is fast and lawyer-free. For larger sums, a breach of contract action in civil court is the backstop, but most clients settle once they see you'll actually file. Compare the timelines and costs in [mechanics lien vs. demand letter](/blog/2026-06-02-mechanics-lien-vs-demand-letter-california-contractor).

## The smart order of operations

Document → demand → preserve the lien → invoke penalties → file if you must. Each step costs more time and money than the last, which is why a sharp attorney demand letter, sent early with the statutes cited, resolves the large majority of withheld-payment disputes before a contractor ever steps into a courtroom.
