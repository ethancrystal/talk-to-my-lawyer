# Construction Cost Overruns in California: When You Must Pay More Than the Estimate — and When You Don't

**Short answer:** Whether you owe a cost overrun in California depends on your contract. A signed fixed-price home improvement contract generally locks the price unless you approved written change orders (Business & Professions Code § 7159). Extra charges with no signed change order — and especially charges above a contractor's own written estimate without your consent — are often unenforceable.

You agreed to a kitchen remodel for $48,000. The final invoice says $61,000. The contractor shrugs and says "things came up." Before you write that check, understand this: California regulates home improvement contracts tightly, and a contractor cannot simply bill you more because the job turned out harder than expected. Whether you owe the overage comes down to what your contract says and whether the extras were properly authorized.

## What kind of contract do you have?

The first question is whether your job was priced as a **fixed (lump-sum) contract** or a **cost-plus / time-and-materials** contract.

- **Fixed-price:** You agreed to one total number for a defined scope. The contractor bears the risk that the work costs more than expected. You generally owe more only for changes you authorized.
- **Cost-plus / time-and-materials:** You agreed to pay actual costs plus a markup or fee. Overruns are more likely to be owed — but the contractor still must charge reasonably, document actual costs, and honor any "not-to-exceed" cap in the contract.

Most residential remodels are fixed-price, which is exactly why an unexplained overrun should raise your guard.

## What does California require in a home improvement contract?

For home improvement work, Business & Professions Code § 7159 requires a **written contract** with specific contents: a description of the work and materials, the total contract price, a schedule of payments, and required consumer notices. Two protections matter most for overruns:

- **Down payment cap.** The down payment cannot exceed the lesser of **10% of the contract price or $1,000**. A contractor demanding far more up front is already out of compliance.
- **Change orders in writing.** Any change to the scope or price is supposed to be documented in a written change order that both parties sign, stating the added cost. This is the heart of overrun disputes.

A contractor who skips the written contract or the change-order process is on weak legal ground when demanding extra money.

## When do you actually owe an overrun?

You generally owe the extra amount when:

- You signed a **written change order** approving the additional work and its price.
- Your contract is genuinely **cost-plus** and the charges reflect reasonable, documented actual costs within any cap.
- You requested extra work and clearly agreed to pay for it, even if the paperwork was informal (though written authorization is far stronger for the contractor).

In these situations, refusing to pay for work you approved can itself be a breach of contract.

## When is an overrun unenforceable?

You often do **not** owe the extra amount when:

- The contractor performed extra work **without a signed change order** on a fixed-price job and simply added it to the bill.
- The charges exceed the contractor's **own written estimate** with no authorization for the increase.
- The "extras" are really part of the original scope the contractor underbid.
- The work is **defective**, and the overrun is the cost of redoing the contractor's own mistakes.

California's consumer-protection framework, plus basic contract law, means a contractor generally can't unilaterally raise a fixed price after the fact. The burden is on the contractor to show you authorized the additional cost.

## What about a contractor who was unlicensed?

If the contractor wasn't properly licensed for the work, the calculus changes dramatically. Under Business & Professions Code § 7031, an unlicensed contractor generally **cannot sue to collect** at all — and you may even be able to recover amounts already paid. Always verify the license on the CSLB website. See [I hired an unlicensed contractor in California](/blog/2026-06-02-hired-unlicensed-contractor-california-get-money-back).

## How should you respond to an inflated final bill?

1. **Pull your paperwork.** Compare the signed contract price, any signed change orders, and the contractor's original estimate against the final invoice. Identify exactly which line items are new and whether you authorized them.
2. **Ask for written justification.** Request an itemized accounting of every charge above the contract price and the signed change order authorizing each one. Legitimate extras can be documented; padded ones usually can't.
3. **Pay what you clearly owe; dispute the rest in writing.** Don't pay the disputed amount just to avoid friction — and don't refuse the whole bill if part is legitimate. A written dispute that separates the two is your strongest position.
4. **Send a demand or dispute letter.** A letter that cites § 7159, the change-order requirement, and the original estimate puts the contractor on notice that you know the rules. A [breach of contract demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) framework works here.

## What if the contractor threatens a mechanics lien?

A contractor may threaten to record a mechanics lien for the disputed amount. A lien clouds your title — but it isn't proof you owe the money. If the lien is for unauthorized overruns, you can dispute it, demand a release, and (if needed) petition the court to remove it; California penalizes claimants who refuse to release a lien they're not entitled to (Civil Code § 8480, § 8482). See [a contractor filed a mechanics lien on my house](/blog/2026-06-02-contractor-filed-mechanics-lien-on-my-house-california).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a written estimate binding in California?** An estimate isn't automatically a fixed price, but a contractor generally can't exceed it without your authorization — and on a fixed-price home improvement contract, the contract price controls unless you signed change orders.

**Can a contractor charge more for "unforeseen conditions"?** Only if your contract allows it or you approve a change order. Genuinely hidden conditions (e.g., rot discovered behind a wall) are common, but the contractor still must get your written approval for the added cost before billing it.

**How long do I have to dispute or sue over a contract?** Written contracts in California carry a four-year statute of limitations (Code of Civil Procedure § 337). Don't sit on a dispute, but you have time to gather documents and send a demand first.

**Do I need a lawyer?** For a clear, documented dispute, a flat-fee attorney demand letter is often enough to resolve an inflated bill without court. For larger or defect-related disputes, a consultation helps you weigh small claims (up to $12,500 for an individual) against civil litigation.

## What does a legitimate change order look like?

Knowing what a proper change order contains makes it easy to spot a padded bill. A valid change order should be **in writing**, **signed by both parties before the extra work is done**, and should state (1) a clear description of the added or deleted work, (2) the specific change in price — as a dollar amount, not a vague "plus costs," and (3) any change to the completion date. When a contractor hands you a stack of "extras" at the end with no signed authorizations, they're asking you to ratify charges you never agreed to. You're generally within your rights to refuse the unauthorized portion while paying for the original scope and any changes you actually signed off on.

Verbal approvals are the gray zone. If you genuinely told the contractor "yes, add the skylight, I understand it costs more," a court may find you agreed — but the contractor carries the burden of proving it. Written change orders exist precisely to remove that ambiguity, which is why their absence usually favors the homeowner in a fixed-price dispute.

## Common cost-overrun scenarios in California

**The underbid.** A contractor wins the job with a low number, then "discovers" the real cost mid-project. If the scope didn't change, this is the contractor's risk on a fixed-price contract — not yours.

**The genuine hidden condition.** Rot behind a wall, outdated wiring, or unexpected soil issues are real. The contractor can charge for addressing them — but still needs your written authorization for the added cost before billing it.

**The scope creep you invited.** You kept adding requests — better tile, an extra outlet, a bigger island. Here you likely owe the extras, though the amounts should be reasonable and documented.

**The defect disguised as an extra.** Watch for "extra" charges that are really the cost of fixing the contractor's own mistakes. You don't owe a contractor to redo work they did wrong.

Sorting your dispute into the right scenario tells you how much leverage you have before you respond.

## How does prejudgment interest and the statute of limitations affect this?

If a dispute ends up in court, two numbers matter. California allows **10% annual prejudgment interest** on a liquidated contract debt (Civil Code § 3289) — which can cut both ways depending on who is found to owe whom. And written contracts carry a **four-year statute of limitations** (Code of Civil Procedure § 337), so neither side can indefinitely delay. These facts are worth citing in a dispute letter because they show you understand the real stakes and aren't bluffing.

A cost overrun is not automatically a debt. In California, the contractor who wants more than the contract price has to show you agreed to it — so slow down, compare the paperwork, and make them prove every dollar above the number you signed.
