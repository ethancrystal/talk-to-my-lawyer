# Demand Letter vs. Cease and Desist vs. Settlement Letter: Which One Do You Actually Need?

You searched "demand letter" and got back a dozen different terms — demand letter, cease and desist, settlement letter, breach of contract letter, pre-litigation letter — all on attorney letterhead, all asking the recipient to do something. Are they the same? No.

**Short answer:** A demand letter asks for something you're owed, usually money. A cease and desist letter tells someone to stop doing something harmful, usually conduct. A settlement letter formalizes terms to resolve a dispute that's already on the table or in front of a court. The legal goals are different, and sending the wrong one wastes the strongest tool you have: the first letter.

Here's how to tell them apart and pick the right one.

## What does a demand letter actually do?

A demand letter is a written request, signed by an attorney, asking the recipient to do something specific — most often to pay money that's owed under a contract, an invoice, or a legal duty. It states the facts, cites the legal basis, sets a deadline, and tells the recipient what happens if the deadline is missed.

In California, demand letters are routine in:

- Unpaid invoices and breach of contract
- Security deposit disputes between landlords and tenants
- Construction defects and contractor non-completion
- Personal injury claims (sent to insurance carriers)
- Consumer refunds the seller is refusing to honor

The demand letter creates a paper trail. If the recipient ignores it and you later file suit, that letter often becomes Exhibit A. California courts also expect parties to attempt informal resolution before filing — a documented demand demonstrates you did.

Choose a [demand letter](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices) when the question is *"pay what you owe me."*

## When is a cease and desist letter the right tool?

A cease and desist letter (often called a C&D) tells the recipient to stop a specific activity — and usually to confirm in writing that they will not resume it. The letter is appropriate when the harm is ongoing rather than fixed in the past.

Common California use cases:

- Trademark infringement (someone using your brand or logo)
- Copyright infringement (someone selling or hosting your protected work)
- Online defamation that's still being posted
- Counterfeit goods listings on ecommerce platforms
- Harassment, repeated contact, or unwanted solicitation

A [cease and desist letter](/blog/2026-05-13-what-is-a-trademark-cease-and-desist-letter) is often a precursor to a lawsuit for injunctive relief — meaning if the recipient doesn't stop, the next step is asking a court to order them to stop. The letter itself isn't a court order, but it does establish that the recipient was put on notice, which strengthens any later claim for damages.

Choose a C&D when the question is *"stop doing this."*

## What is a settlement letter, and when does it come into play?

A settlement letter (sometimes called a settlement offer or settlement proposal) is different from the first two: it assumes a dispute already exists and proposes specific terms to resolve it. It's a negotiating document, not an opening demand.

Settlement letters appear after a demand letter has been answered, after a complaint has been filed, or during mediation. They typically include a proposed dollar figure, a release of claims, confidentiality terms, and a payment schedule. In California, written settlement communications made for the purpose of resolving a dispute are generally protected by Evidence Code § 1152 — meaning they usually can't be used against you in court as admissions of liability.

A settlement letter is the right tool only when both sides are already in some form of negotiation. Sending one to a party that hasn't acknowledged the dispute is usually premature — they may simply pocket the offer as a starting point and respond with nothing.

## Quick decision guide: which letter does your situation call for?

Ask yourself two questions.

**Question 1: What do you want them to do?**

- Pay me → demand letter
- Stop doing something → cease and desist
- Agree to terms that end this dispute → settlement letter

**Question 2: Where are you in the process?**

- First written escalation → demand letter or C&D
- Mid-negotiation, or post-complaint → settlement letter
- Repeated harm despite earlier warnings → C&D, often paired with a demand for damages

If your situation is mixed — for example, a contractor abandoned a job *and* is now using your business name to mislead customers — you may need both a demand letter and a cease and desist letter, sometimes combined into a single document. An attorney drafting your letter will identify this in the intake.

## When a letter is the right tool

If you can name exactly what you want the other side to do — pay, stop, or settle — you're ready for one of these letters. Pick wrong and you waste the leverage of a first, well-aimed letter. Pick right and you often resolve the matter without filing in court.
