# Mechanics Lien vs. Demand Letter: Which Gets a California Contractor Paid Faster?

**Short answer:** A demand letter is faster, cheaper, and best as your first move — it often gets payment in days without recording anything. A mechanics lien is stronger leverage but has strict deadlines and only works if you preserved your rights. The smartest approach for most California contractors is to send the demand letter first and keep the lien as the credible next step.

When a client won't pay, contractors usually reach for one of two tools. Choosing the right one — and the right order — saves weeks and protects money you've already earned.

## What does a demand letter do?

A demand letter is a written notice that states what you're owed, references your contract, and gives a deadline to pay before you escalate. It costs little, can go out the same day, and resolves a large share of disputes on its own because it signals you're organized and serious. An attorney-signed version adds weight without committing you to litigation.

**Best when:** the relationship isn't fully broken, the amount is documented, or you want to resolve things before incurring lien or court costs.

## What does a mechanics lien do?

A mechanics lien attaches to the property's **title**, which can block a sale or refinance until it's cleared. That makes it powerful leverage — but it comes with rules:

- You generally must have served a **20-day preliminary notice** to have lien rights as a sub or supplier (Civil Code § 8200).
- You must record within your window — about 90 days after completion for a direct contractor, or 60 days after a recorded Notice of Completion (Civil Code § 8412–8414).
- You must file a **foreclosure lawsuit within 90 days of recording** or the lien lapses (Civil Code § 8460).

**Best when:** the client is ignoring you, owns the property, and the amount justifies the formality — and when you've preserved your deadlines.

## Head-to-head: speed, cost, and leverage

| Factor | Demand letter | Mechanics lien |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed to send** | Same day | Requires recording + (often) a preliminary notice |
| **Cost** | Low (especially flat-fee) | Recording fees + likely attorney/foreclosure costs |
| **Leverage** | Moderate — pressure to pay | High — clouds title, blocks sale/refi |
| **Deadlines** | None to start | Strict; missing one can void your rights |
| **Best as** | First move | Escalation / backstop |

## Can you use both?

Yes — and you usually should. A [Notice of Intent to Lien](/blog/2026-06-02-what-is-a-notice-of-intent-to-lien-california) blends the two: it's a demand letter that explicitly warns a lien is coming. You get the speed and low cost of a letter with the title-clouding threat of a lien behind it. If the deadline passes unpaid, you record the lien you already warned about.

## What about prejudgment interest and penalties?

Both paths can recover more than the base debt. California allows **10% annual prejudgment interest** on a liquidated contract debt (Civil Code § 3289), and prompt-payment statutes such as Business & Professions Code § 7108.5 can add penalties when payment is wrongfully withheld down the contracting chain. Including these in your demand raises the cost of the client doing nothing.

## The recommendation

For most California contractors owed a final payment, **lead with the demand letter** — ideally one that doubles as a Notice of Intent to Lien — and treat the recorded lien and any lawsuit as the escalation if the deadline passes. You capture the speed and low cost of a letter while keeping your strongest leverage in reserve.
