# The Complete Guide to Contractor Disputes and Construction Defect Claims in California

California's contractor licensing system is among the most comprehensive in the nation, and it gives homeowners, property managers, and business owners powerful tools to resolve disputes with contractors who don't deliver what they promised. This guide covers the full landscape — from understanding your rights under the law to choosing the right enforcement strategy for your specific situation.

If you're dealing with a contractor who did poor work, abandoned a project, overcharged you, or damaged your property, this is the reference you need.

## What California Law Says About Contractor Obligations

California regulates contractors primarily through the Contractors State License Law, codified in Business & Professions Code §7000 et seq. This body of law establishes who must be licensed, what standards they must meet, and what happens when they fall short.

Under B&P Code §7109, willful departure from or disregard of plans and specifications constitutes a cause for disciplinary action. Under B&P Code §7106, willful or fraudulent acts in the capacity of a contractor are grounds for license suspension or revocation. And under B&P Code §7107, abandonment of a construction project is independently actionable.

For residential construction specifically, Civil Code §895 through §945.5 — commonly known as the "Right to Repair Act" or SB 800 — sets detailed performance standards for every building component. These standards cover structural integrity, soil and drainage, plumbing and sewer, electrical systems, waterproofing, and more. If the construction fails to meet these standards, the homeowner has a statutory claim regardless of what the contract says.

The practical takeaway: California law provides multiple overlapping frameworks for holding contractors accountable. You're rarely limited to a single theory of recovery.

## What Are the Most Common Types of Contractor Disputes?

Contractor disputes in California generally fall into five categories, each with its own legal nuances.

**Defective workmanship** is the most common. The contractor completed the work but it doesn't meet trade standards, building codes, or contract specifications. Maybe the tile is uneven, the roof leaks, or the electrical work fails inspection. Under B&P Code §7109 and the standards in Civil Code §896, defective work is actionable whether the contractor meant to cut corners or simply lacked competence.

**Project abandonment** occurs when a contractor stops showing up before the work is finished. Under B&P Code §7107, abandonment without legal excuse is a cause for license discipline. From the homeowner's perspective, it also constitutes a breach of contract actionable under Civil Code §3300 for damages.

**Overcharging and change order fraud** happens when a contractor bills for work not performed, inflates material costs, or pushes unnecessary change orders. Home improvement contracts are specifically regulated under B&P Code §7159, which requires detailed written contracts for residential jobs over $500 and restricts down payments to $1,000 or 10% of the contract price, whichever is less.

**Property damage** — a contractor damages your existing property during the course of work. This creates both a contract claim and a potential tort claim for negligence.

**Failure to obtain required permits** — a contractor who skips required permits exposes you to fines and complications when you sell. Under B&P Code §7090, the CSLB can discipline contractors who fail to obtain required permits.

## What Are Your Deadlines for Taking Action?

Statutes of limitations are the invisible clock running on every contractor dispute. Miss the deadline and you lose your right to sue, no matter how strong your case is.

For **written contracts**, California Code of Civil Procedure §337 gives you four years from the breach to file suit. For **oral contracts**, CCP §339 gives you two years. These deadlines typically run from when you discovered the breach (or reasonably should have discovered it), not from when the contract was signed.

**Construction defect claims** under SB 800 (Civil Code §895 et seq.) have their own deadlines that vary by defect type. Structural defects carry a ten-year window. Plumbing and electrical defects: four years. Exterior finish issues like paint and stucco: two years. These are measured from the date of substantial completion of the original construction.

For **latent defects** — problems that aren't visible on inspection — CCP §337.15 sets an absolute outside limit of ten years from substantial completion for most claims. The discovery rule can extend shorter deadlines but cannot extend past this ten-year cap.

The practical lesson: act quickly. Even if you're within the legal deadline, evidence degrades, contractors move or close their businesses, and memories fade. The best time to send a demand letter is as soon as you've identified and documented the problem.

## How Does the CSLB Complaint Process Work?

The Contractors State License Board accepts complaints online, by mail, or by phone. The process has several stages, and understanding them helps you set realistic expectations.

**Stage 1: Filing.** You submit a complaint with your contract, documentation of the dispute, photos of defective work, and records of communication with the contractor. The more complete your documentation, the faster the investigation moves.

**Stage 2: Investigation.** The CSLB assigns an Enforcement Representative who contacts the contractor and may schedule an onsite inspection. Under B&P Code §7011, the investigator has authority to examine the work, review records, and interview witnesses. This stage typically takes 60–120 days.

**Stage 3: Resolution or escalation.** If the investigation confirms a violation, the CSLB may offer an informal settlement, order the contractor to make restitution, issue a citation with a fine, or refer the case for formal disciplinary proceedings. In serious cases, the CSLB can suspend or revoke the contractor's license.

One important note: the CSLB process is regulatory, not judicial. The CSLB can order restitution and discipline the contractor, but it cannot award you damages for pain and suffering, lost use of your property, or other consequential losses. For those, you need a demand letter, small claims court, or civil litigation.

## What Role Does a Demand Letter Play in Contractor Disputes?

A [demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) is the most cost-effective first step in almost every contractor dispute. It serves three critical functions simultaneously.

First, it satisfies California's notice requirements. Under Civil Code §1492, a party must generally demand performance before suing for breach. The demand letter checks this box.

Second, it creates leverage. A contractor who receives a well-drafted letter citing specific contract provisions, building code violations, and California statutes understands they're dealing with someone who knows their rights. Most contractors will negotiate at this point because the cost of settling is a fraction of the cost of defending a lawsuit.

Third, it establishes a record. If the dispute goes to court, the demand letter shows the judge that you acted reasonably and gave the contractor every opportunity to make things right. California courts consistently look favorably on parties who attempted informal resolution before filing suit.

## What About the Contractor's License Bond?

Every licensed contractor in California must maintain a contractor's license bond under B&P Code §7071.6. The current minimum bond amount is $25,000 for most contractor classifications, though some specialty categories require higher amounts.

This bond exists specifically to protect consumers. If a contractor causes you financial harm through faulty work, abandonment, or fraud, you can file a claim directly with the surety company that issued the bond. You don't need a court order. You need documentation of your damages and a properly prepared claim.

The bond claim process works like this: identify the surety company through the CSLB's license lookup tool, submit a written claim with supporting documentation, and the surety investigates. If the claim is valid, the surety pays up to the bond amount. The contractor then owes the surety company for the payout — creating additional pressure on them to resolve disputes before bond claims are filed.

One strategic note: mention the bond claim option in your demand letter. Contractors who might ignore a letter about potential lawsuits pay attention when their bond — and by extension their license — is at stake.

## What Special Rules Apply to Unlicensed Contractors?

Unlicensed contracting is a significant problem in California, and the law comes down hard on it — in your favor.

Under B&P Code §7028, contracting without a license when one is required is a misdemeanor punishable by fines up to $15,000 and up to six months in county jail for repeat offenders. But the civil remedy is where homeowners really benefit.

B&P Code §7031(b) allows any person who uses the services of an unlicensed contractor to bring an action to recover all compensation paid. The California Supreme Court in *Hidrogo v. Superior Court* confirmed this means everything — not just the excess over fair value, but every dollar paid. The unlicensed contractor has no right to keep any payment for their work.

This creates an extraordinary recovery position. If you paid $30,000 to an unlicensed contractor and the work was worth $20,000, you can still recover the full $30,000. You can verify any contractor's license status for free on the CSLB website before starting work — and you should.

## How Much Does It Cost to Resolve a Contractor Dispute?

The cost depends entirely on which resolution path you choose.

**CSLB complaint:** Free. No filing fee, no attorney required.

**Demand letter (traditional attorney):** $500 to $2,000 depending on complexity and the attorney's hourly rate.

**Demand letter (flat-fee service):** Significantly less. Talk to My Lawyer offers flat-fee legal letters, and your first letter is free.

**Small claims court:** Filing fees range from $30 to $75 depending on the claim amount. No attorney is allowed in California small claims court, so there are no legal fees. The jurisdiction cap is $12,500 for individuals.

**Civil litigation:** Attorney fees for a construction defect case can run from $10,000 to $100,000 or more depending on complexity. This is the option of last resort for most homeowners, but it may be necessary for large claims or when the contractor's insurance is involved.

The most cost-effective strategy for disputes under $12,500 is almost always: demand letter first, then small claims if the letter doesn't resolve it. For larger disputes, a demand letter followed by a CSLB complaint and bond claim covers most of the enforcement territory without incurring litigation costs.

## What Steps Should You Take When a Contractor Dispute Arises?

A clear sequence of actions gives you the best chance of resolution at the lowest cost.

**Step 1: Document everything.** Before you send a single message, photograph and video the defective work. Collect your contract, all receipts, invoices, change orders, permits (or lack thereof), and every text, email, and voicemail with the contractor. Get a written estimate from another licensed contractor for the cost to repair or complete the work.

**Step 2: Send a formal demand letter.** Lay out the breach, cite the relevant contract terms and California statutes, state your damages, and set a clear deadline. This alone resolves the majority of contractor disputes.

**Step 3: File a CSLB complaint.** If the contractor doesn't respond to the demand letter, file with the CSLB. The investigation creates additional pressure and may result in ordered restitution.

**Step 4: File a bond claim.** If direct resolution fails, go after the contractor's license bond. This is particularly effective for damages under the bond amount.

**Step 5: Small claims or civil court.** If informal remedies don't work, file suit. Small claims court handles disputes up to $12,500 quickly and without attorneys. Larger claims require civil litigation.

The key to this entire process is starting with the right documentation and a strong demand letter. Everything that follows builds on that foundation.
