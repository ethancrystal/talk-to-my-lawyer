# Does a Demand Letter Need to Be Notarized in California?

**Short answer: No. There is no California statute requiring a demand letter to be notarized. Notarization adds nothing to a demand letter's legal force. What makes a letter effective is its content (specific claim, amount, deadline, legal basis) and proof of delivery — not a notary seal.**

This question comes up because people conflate "official" with "notarized." Notarization verifies identity for documents where signature authenticity matters — deeds, affidavits, powers of attorney. A demand letter isn't any of those things. It's a communication that says "you owe me money, here's why, pay by this date."

## What Notarization Actually Does (and Doesn't Do)

Under California Government Code § 8202, a notary public verifies the identity of the person signing a document. That's it. The notary doesn't verify the contents are true, doesn't make the document "more legal," and doesn't add any enforceable weight.

For a demand letter, identity verification is irrelevant. The recipient already knows who you are — you're the person they owe money to. Adding a notary seal doesn't make your $3,000 claim more valid or your 30-day deadline more binding.

> **California Government Code § 8202** — A notary public authenticates signatures on documents. The notary does not validate the truthfulness or legal sufficiency of the document's contents. A demand letter's force comes from the legal claim it describes, not from who witnessed the signature.


## What Actually Makes a Demand Letter Legally Effective

Three things matter. Notarization isn't one of them:

| Element | Why It Matters | Notarization Substitute |
|---------|---------------|------------------------|
| Specific factual claim | Tells recipient exactly what they owe and why | No |
| Cited legal basis | Shows a real statute or contract supports your claim | No |
| Proof of delivery | Proves they received it (critical if you later file suit) | Certified mail return receipt |
| Attorney signature | Signals a real lawsuit is next (increases response rate 2-3x) | No |
| Clear deadline | Creates urgency and a documented "last chance" | No |

Certified mail with return receipt (USPS Form 3811) provides proof of delivery — which you'll actually need in court. A notary stamp provides proof someone watched you sign, which nobody will ask about.

## When People Waste Money on Notarization

At $15-$25 per document (California's maximum notary fee under Gov. Code § 8211), notarizing a demand letter is throwing money at a placebo. That same $15 covers certified mail postage plus return receipt — which provides something a court actually cares about: proof the recipient got the letter.

## The One Exception: Sworn Statements

If your demand letter includes a sworn declaration of facts — for example, an attached affidavit stating the work you performed and the amount unpaid — that declaration portion could benefit from notarization because it becomes admissible as a sworn statement under penalty of perjury (CCP § 2015.5). But the demand letter itself remains an unsworn business communication regardless.

## What to Do Instead of Notarizing

Send your demand letter via certified mail with return receipt requested. Keep a copy of everything. If an attorney signs it, that signature — on letterhead with a California State Bar number — carries more weight than any notary seal. The attorney is staking their professional license on the claim's legitimacy.
