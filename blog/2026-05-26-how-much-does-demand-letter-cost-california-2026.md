A demand letter in California costs anywhere from zero dollars to about $2,000. That spread isn't because some letters are twenty times more powerful than others — most working demand letters look structurally similar. The spread is because the legal industry sells the same product through four different billing models, and the difference between $0 and $2,000 is which model you bought from.

**Short answer:** California demand letters cost $0 (DIY), $25–$50 (online template service), $150–$300 (flat-fee unbundled service like TTML), or $500–$2,000+ (hourly traditional firm). The right price depends entirely on the dispute amount, the opposing party, and how clean the legal theory is.

> **California Code of Civil Procedure § 116.230** — The court filing fee a demand letter is implicitly compared against. Small claims fees range from **$30** (claims under $1,500) to **$75** (claims $5,000–$10,000) to **$100** (claims $10,000+ filed by frequent filers). Add lost-wages for a hearing day and your effective small-claims cost is $200–$500 — which is the cost bar any demand letter needs to clear to be the cheaper move.

## The four pricing models

### Model 1: DIY ($0–$15)

You write the letter yourself. The cost is the certified-mail postage — about $9 with return receipt at any USPS counter — plus maybe a few dollars for letterhead paper if you're feeling formal.

This model works when the dispute is small (under $1,000), the other side is generally responsive, and the legal theory is obvious. A self-written letter to a client who's been responsive but slow will often produce payment. The same self-written letter sent to a party who already ignored you once usually produces another round of silence.

The hidden cost of DIY isn't dollars — it's time and risk. You'll spend two to four hours researching the right statute, drafting, revising, and sending. And whatever you write becomes a piece of evidence if the dispute escalates. A poorly written self-letter can lock in bad facts that a defense attorney later uses against you.

### Model 2: Online template service ($25–$50)

LegalZoom, Rocket Lawyer, and a half-dozen other consumer-legal sites sell fill-in-the-blank demand letter templates. You answer some questions, the system generates a letter, you print and mail it.

The product is essentially a slightly-better DIY. The letter has the right structure, uses some legal-sounding language, and prints on something resembling letterhead. It is not signed by an attorney. It does not represent legal advice. The recipient knows or quickly figures this out — there's no attorney name or bar number anywhere on the document.

For under-$1,000 disputes, this can be worth the modest spend. For anything larger, the product underperforms its price. The letter looks legal-adjacent but doesn't actually have an attorney standing behind it.

### Model 3: Flat-fee unbundled service ($150–$300)

A flat-fee service — TTML at $199, or similar unbundled-legal services in California — sells one specific deliverable: an attorney-drafted, attorney-signed letter on law firm letterhead, mailed certified to the recipient. The fee is fixed before the work starts. There's no retainer, no hourly billing, no surprises on the back end.

What you're paying for, structurally:

- A licensed California attorney's review of your facts and documentation.
- Identification of the applicable California statute (Civil Code § 1950.5, Business and Professions Code § 7108.5, etc.).
- A drafted letter that complies with California pre-litigation conventions.
- The attorney's signature, bar number, and firm address — the signals that change how the recipient treats the document.
- Certified mailing with return receipt.

The flat-fee model exists because there's a large class of California disputes — roughly $1,000 to $25,000 — that are too small for traditional firms to economically take, but large enough that DIY undersells the recovery. The flat fee is what lets unbundled services serve that middle.

### Model 4: Traditional firm hourly ($500–$2,000+)

A traditional California law firm bills the demand letter against a retainer. Partner hourly rates in California typically run $400–$800 in 2026; associate rates run $250–$450. A demand letter usually takes 2 to 5 hours of attorney time depending on complexity. Math it out and you're at $500–$4,000 for the same document the flat-fee service produced.

What you get for the premium:

- More attorney attention. The partner reviews the file, the associate drafts, the partner edits. The letter has been seen by more legal eyes.
- A firm with deeper resources to follow through if the dispute escalates. The same partner who signed your letter can file the lawsuit a month later.
- A name recognized in legal circles. For high-stakes disputes — a $500,000 commercial breach, a major executive employment dispute — recipient counsel knows the firm and responds differently.

For most disputes between $1,000 and $25,000, the traditional firm model is overpriced for the marginal benefit. Past about $75,000 in dispute value, or when litigation is the genuine next step, traditional firms are the right call.

## The math: when each model pays for itself

A demand letter is an investment that should produce a return. The simplest test: what percentage of disputes does each model resolve, and is the expected recovery greater than the cost?

DIY letters resolve about 20-35% of disputes against responsive parties, lower against unresponsive ones. Call it 25% baseline. For a $2,000 dispute, that's $500 expected recovery against a $9 cost — clearly worth it.

Online template letters resolve roughly the same percentage as DIY, maybe a few points higher. For a $2,000 dispute, $500 expected recovery against $50 — still worth it, marginal improvement over DIY.

Flat-fee attorney letters resolve 60-70% of disputes in our observed range. For a $2,000 dispute, that's $1,300 expected recovery against $199 — worth it. For a $10,000 dispute, $6,500 expected recovery against $199 — extremely worth it. For a $500 dispute, $325 expected recovery against $199 — not worth it; DIY makes more sense.

Traditional firm letters resolve at the same or slightly higher rate as flat-fee letters, but cost 5-10x more. For most disputes under $75,000, the marginal increase in success rate doesn't justify the price. Above that threshold, the relationship value matters more than the per-letter math.

## What drives the price within each model

A few variables move the price inside each model:

**Complexity of the legal theory.** A clean breach of contract takes less attorney time than a multi-party dispute involving overlapping statutes. Flat-fee services have a cap on complexity — past a certain point, the dispute is too tangled for the flat-fee product, and the service refers out to traditional counsel.

**Documentation quality.** A client who shows up with a signed contract, three months of email correspondence, and a clean invoice ledger costs less attorney time than a client with text-message screenshots and approximate dates.

**Urgency.** Some firms charge a rush premium for letters sent within 48 hours. Most flat-fee services have a standard turnaround (TTML's is five business days) with no rush option.

**Geography.** California law firm rates are higher in the Bay Area and Los Angeles than in Sacramento or Fresno. The flat-fee model collapses this difference — the price is the price regardless of where the attorney's office is.

## Hidden costs to watch for

A few traps in legal pricing that catch first-time buyers:

**The "free consultation" that isn't.** Some firms offer a free 30-minute consultation but begin billing the moment you sign a representation agreement, including time spent on the consultation itself. Read the engagement letter.

**Retainer minimums.** Traditional firms often require a $3,000-$10,000 retainer to start work, even if the demand letter only consumes $1,500 of that. The remainder can be refunded but sometimes isn't, depending on the engagement terms.

**Six-minute billing increments.** Most California firms bill in 0.1-hour ($25-$80) increments. A two-minute phone call gets billed as six minutes. Over the life of an engagement, this adds 10-20% to the apparent hourly rate.

**Mailing and "administrative" fees.** Some firms charge $15-$50 separately for certified mailing, copies, and filing. Flat-fee services typically bundle this in.

## How this compares to the alternatives

Compared to filing in small claims court directly, the demand letter is usually cheaper. Small claims filing fees in California range from $30 (claims under $1,500) to $75 (claims over $5,000) under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.230. Add a day of lost wages for the hearing, and your effective cost of filing is $200-$500. A flat-fee demand letter often resolves the dispute before any of that becomes necessary.

## The decision tree, day by day

- **Day 0** — Sit down. Pull the contract. Calculate exact damages including 10% interest under Civ. Code § 3289.
- **Day 1** — Categorize the dispute by the table above. Pick the model where expected recovery > cost.
- **Day 2** — DIY: draft and send. Template: buy and customize. Flat-fee: 10-min intake form. Traditional firm: schedule consultation.
- **Day 3–7** — Letter mailed (timing varies by model — DIY mails same-day; flat-fee turns in ~5 business days; firms take 1–3 weeks).
- **Day 10–15** — Recipient receives. Deadline clock starts.
- **Day 25–35** — Deadline passes. Pay, negotiate, or decide to escalate.
- **Day 35+** — If unresolved, decision: small claims, civil suit, or accept the loss.

## Picking the right model

A simple decision framework:

- Dispute under $1,000, responsive other side → DIY or template service.
- Dispute $1,000-$5,000, party who already ignored you once → flat-fee attorney letter.
- Dispute $5,000-$25,000 → flat-fee attorney letter, almost always.
- Dispute $25,000-$75,000 → flat-fee for the first move; traditional firm if escalating.
- Dispute over $75,000 → traditional firm from the start.

Most of the cases we see at TTML fall in the $1,000-$25,000 range, which is the sweet spot for unbundled work. Smaller disputes don't justify attorney fees; larger ones often justify hourly engagement from the start.

## Bottom line

The price of a California demand letter isn't really a number — it's a structural choice about how you want to buy legal work. Four pricing models exist because four kinds of disputes exist. Match the model to the dispute, and the letter pays for itself within days of mailing. Mismatch them, and you've either overspent on a firm you didn't need or underspent on a product that won't move the recipient.

The cheapest letter that actually works is almost always the right answer. For most California disputes between $1,000 and $25,000, that's the flat-fee attorney letter at around $199.
