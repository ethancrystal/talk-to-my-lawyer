# How to Write a Demand Letter to an Insurance Company in California (2026): The Complete Guide

**Short Answer:** A demand letter to an insurance company is a written request for fair payment that lays out the facts, the coverage, your damages, and a deadline to respond. In California, citing the Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations and the insurer's duty of good faith gives the letter real leverage.

Whether your claim was lowballed, delayed, or denied, a well-built demand letter is often what turns an insurer's "no" into a reasonable settlement. This guide walks through how to write one that an adjuster takes seriously — and the California laws that make a strong letter hard to ignore.

## What is a demand letter to an insurer, and when do you send one?

A demand letter formally states what you believe the insurer owes and asks them to pay it by a deadline. You typically send one when:

- The insurer denied a claim you believe is covered.
- The settlement offer is far below your documented losses.
- The insurer is dragging out the process or not responding.
- You're ready to resolve the matter but want to avoid filing suit first.

It works for first-party claims (your own policy — property, auto, health, disability) and third-party claims (against someone else's liability insurer, such as after a car accident).

## What laws give a California demand letter its leverage?

California regulates insurer conduct closely, and naming those rules signals you know your rights:

- **Fair Claims Settlement Practices Regulations (10 Cal. Code Regs. § 2695 et seq.).** These require insurers to acknowledge claims promptly, investigate thoroughly, and explain denials in writing.
- **Insurance Code § 790.03.** This lists unfair claims practices, such as misrepresenting policy provisions and failing to attempt prompt, fair settlement once liability is clear.
- **The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.** When an insurer unreasonably denies or delays a valid claim, it can be liable for "bad faith" — exposing it to damages beyond the policy benefits, and potentially punitive damages.

A letter that frames an unreasonable denial as potential bad faith changes the insurer's risk calculation, because bad-faith exposure can far exceed the claim itself.

## How do I value my claim before writing?

Your demand number should be built, not guessed. Separate your losses into categories and document each:

- **Economic damages** — repair or replacement costs, medical bills, lost wages, and other out-of-pocket losses, each backed by invoices, estimates, or records.
- **General damages** — for injury claims, pain and suffering and other non-economic harm, supported by the nature and duration of your injuries.
- **Policy limits** — know the coverage available, since it caps what you can recover from that policy.

Build a clear, itemized total. An insurer is far more likely to respond to "$18,420 in documented losses, itemized below" than to a round number with no support.

## What should the demand letter include?

A persuasive insurance demand letter follows a clear structure:

1. **Your policy and claim information** — policy number, claim number, date of loss, and the parties.
2. **A factual narrative** — what happened, in plain chronological order, without exaggeration.
3. **The coverage analysis** — the specific policy provisions that make this a covered loss, and a direct response to any reason the insurer gave for denial or reduction.
4. **An itemized damages calculation** — every loss category with supporting documentation referenced or attached.
5. **The legal framework** — a measured reference to the Fair Claims regulations and the duty of good faith, noting the consequences of an unreasonable denial.
6. **A specific demand and deadline** — the exact amount you're demanding and a reasonable response window, often 15–30 days.
7. **A professional close** — stating your willingness to resolve the matter and your readiness to escalate if necessary.

Attach copies (never originals) of your evidence: estimates, photos, bills, records, and the denial letter.

## How do I invoke "bad faith" without overreaching?

Tone matters. You don't threaten or insult; you document. The most effective approach is to lay out the facts and coverage so clearly that an unreasonable denial becomes obvious, then calmly note that California law imposes a duty of good faith and that continued unreasonable handling could constitute bad faith under Insurance Code § 790.03 and the Fair Claims regulations. Let the strength of the documentation imply the consequences. Empty threats weaken a letter; a tightly evidenced one speaks for itself.

## What deadline should I give — and what about time-limited demands?

A reasonable deadline (15–30 days for most claims) creates urgency without looking unreasonable. In some third-party liability situations, attorneys use carefully constructed "time-limited" policy-limits demands that can increase the insurer's exposure if it fails to settle within limits. These are powerful but technical, and a poorly drafted one can backfire — another reason to have an attorney involved in higher-value claims.

## Common mistakes that sink insurance demand letters

- **Exaggerating losses.** Inflated numbers destroy credibility and hand the adjuster a reason to discount everything.
- **Leaving out documentation.** An unsupported demand is easy to ignore. Evidence is leverage.
- **Being vague about the amount.** "Fair compensation" invites a lowball. Name a specific, justified figure.
- **Missing the deadline to sue.** Many policies contain short contractual limitations periods, and statutory deadlines vary by claim type. Confirm yours before you rely on negotiation.
- **Admitting fault or guessing.** Stick to documented facts; don't speculate about liability or your own conduct.

## What happens after I send it?

Expect one of three outcomes: the insurer accepts and pays, makes a counteroffer that opens negotiation, or refuses. A counteroffer is the most common — and it's progress, because it means the letter moved the claim from "no" to "how much." If the insurer refuses outright despite strong documentation, you've built a clean record for a Department of Insurance complaint or a lawsuit, including a potential bad-faith claim. To understand your full menu of options after a denial, read our guide to [what to do when your insurance claim is denied in California](/blog/2026-05-30-insurance-claim-denied-california-options).

## Should the letter come from an attorney?

You can write your own, and for a small, well-documented first-party claim that may be enough. But for denied claims, large losses, or any hint of bad faith, an attorney-signed letter carries decisive weight: it tells the insurer a lawyer has evaluated the claim and is ready to litigate. A flat-fee attorney letter is an affordable way to apply that pressure before committing to a contingency case or a lawsuit.

## The bottom line

A demand letter to an insurance company succeeds on documentation and discipline, not volume or threats. Value your claim carefully, lay out the coverage and the facts, reference California's good-faith duty and Fair Claims regulations, attach your evidence, and set a clear deadline. Do that, and you give the insurer every reason to pay what it owes — and a strong record to escalate if it won't.

## First-party vs. third-party demands: what changes?

The structure above applies to both, but the emphasis shifts. In a **first-party** claim — against your own insurer for property, disability, or similar coverage — the fight is usually about coverage interpretation and the insurer's handling of your claim, so your letter leans hard on the policy language and the Fair Claims regulations. In a **third-party** claim — against the at-fault person's liability insurer — liability and the value of your damages are front and center, so your letter spends more space establishing fault and documenting injury. Knowing which type you have tells you where to put your strongest arguments.

## A short checklist before you hit send

Run through this list before mailing the letter:

- Have you waited until your losses are fully known, so you're not under-valuing the claim?
- Is every dollar in your demand supported by an attached document?
- Did you respond directly to the insurer's stated reason for denial or reduction?
- Did you reference the policy provisions and the good-faith duty without threatening or exaggerating?
- Is there a specific demand amount and a clear deadline?
- Have you confirmed your contractual and statutory deadlines to sue, so negotiation can't run out the clock?
- Did you keep originals and send copies, by a method that proves delivery (such as certified mail)?

Sending by certified mail with return receipt — or another trackable method — gives you proof the insurer received the demand, which matters if you later need to show the timeline of its response.

## What if the insurer just ignores the deadline?

Silence is itself useful evidence. An insurer that fails to respond to a documented, good-faith demand within a reasonable time strengthens a later bad-faith argument, because prompt, fair claim handling is exactly what the Fair Claims regulations require. Keep your file complete — the letter, the proof of delivery, and any follow-ups — and you'll have a clean record for a Department of Insurance complaint or a lawsuit. The same discipline that makes the demand persuasive also makes your escalation credible.
