# Home Improvement Contracts in California: The Legal Rules Every Homeowner and Contractor Should Know (2026)

**Short answer:** California requires most home improvement work over $500 to be performed under a written contract with specific disclosures (Business & Professions Code § 7159). The down payment is capped at the lesser of 10% of the price or $1,000, changes must be documented in signed change orders, and homeowners usually get a 3-day right to cancel. Ignoring these rules creates real legal exposure — and real leverage.

Whether you're a homeowner hiring for a remodel or a contractor running the job, the paperwork is not a formality in California. The state regulates home improvement contracts closely to prevent the abuses that once plagued the industry — oversized deposits, vague scopes, surprise charges. Knowing the rules protects homeowners from being overcharged and protects contractors from disputes that can void their right to get paid.

## When is a written home improvement contract required?

Under Business & Professions Code § 7159, a written contract is required for most home improvement projects where the combined cost of labor and materials is **more than $500**. "Home improvement" broadly covers repairing, remodeling, altering, or adding to a residence and its surrounding property — kitchens, bathrooms, roofing, decks, landscaping, and similar work.

The narrow exemption for "minor work" under Business & Professions Code § 7048 only applies to jobs under $500. In practice, nearly every meaningful project requires both a licensed contractor and a compliant written contract.

## What must the written contract contain?

Section 7159 spells out required contents. A compliant home improvement contract generally must include:

- The **contractor's name, address, and license number**.
- A clear **description of the work** and the materials to be used.
- The **total contract price** stated in dollars.
- A **schedule of payments** tied to work performed or materials delivered.
- The **approximate start and completion dates**.
- Required **statutory notices**, including the down payment and cancellation disclosures and information about the Contractors State License Board.
- A notice about the **Mechanics Lien Warning**, alerting the homeowner that subcontractors and suppliers can lien the property.

A contract missing these elements isn't just sloppy — it can expose the contractor to discipline and weaken their position in a payment dispute.

## How much can a contractor ask for up front?

This is one of the most important consumer protections. The **down payment cannot exceed the lesser of 10% of the contract price or $1,000** (Business & Professions Code § 7159). A $40,000 remodel cannot carry a $10,000 deposit; the cap is $1,000.

After the down payment, further payments must track the work — a contractor generally can't demand large sums far ahead of the labor and materials actually provided. A demand for a giant up-front payment is a classic red flag and, by itself, a violation of California law.

## Do homeowners have a right to cancel?

Often, yes. Home improvement contracts typically come with a **three-business-day right to cancel** for the homeowner, disclosed in the contract itself. Certain buyers — notably **seniors (65 and older)** — may receive an enhanced cancellation window. There are also separate cancellation rights for contracts solicited at the homeowner's residence. The contract must disclose these rights and provide the cancellation form. If a contractor failed to provide proper cancellation notice, the homeowner's cancellation rights can be extended.

## How are changes and extras supposed to be handled?

Through **written change orders**. Any modification to the scope or price should be documented in a change order that both parties sign, stating the added (or reduced) cost. This protects everyone: the homeowner isn't surprised by extra charges, and the contractor has signed proof the homeowner authorized the additional work and price.

The most common construction payment fight starts here — a contractor performs "extras" and bills for them with no signed change order on a fixed-price job. On a fixed-price contract, that overage is frequently unenforceable. See [construction cost overruns in California](/blog/2026-06-02-construction-cost-overruns-california-when-you-must-pay).

## Why the contractor's license matters to the contract

A contract is only as good as the license behind it. Under Business & Professions Code § 7031, an **unlicensed** contractor generally cannot sue to collect for work requiring a license — and the homeowner may recover amounts already paid. Both sides should confirm the license is valid and in the correct classification for the work. Homeowners can verify it on the CSLB website before signing. See [I hired an unlicensed contractor in California](/blog/2026-06-02-hired-unlicensed-contractor-california-get-money-back).

## What protections exist around mechanics liens?

Because subcontractors and suppliers can record a **mechanics lien** against the home even if the homeowner paid the general contractor, the law builds in safeguards:

- The contract's **Mechanics Lien Warning** alerts homeowners to the risk.
- Homeowners can require **conditional and unconditional lien waivers** (Civil Code § 8132–8138) before releasing each payment.
- A wrongfully filed or expired lien must be released on request, and the law penalizes claimants who refuse (Civil Code § 8480, § 8482).

Smart homeowners collect a signed waiver with every check; smart contractors provide them promptly to keep the relationship clean. If a lien is already filed, see [a contractor filed a mechanics lien on my house](/blog/2026-06-02-contractor-filed-mechanics-lien-on-my-house-california).

## What happens when a home improvement contract is breached?

If a contractor abandons the job, does defective work, or overbills, the homeowner's remedies include a demand letter, a CSLB complaint, small claims (up to **$12,500** for an individual), and a civil breach of contract claim. Written contracts carry a **four-year statute of limitations** (Code of Civil Procedure § 337), and a homeowner can often recover **10% annual prejudgment interest** on a liquidated debt (Civil Code § 3289). For contractors who aren't paid, the same contract — if compliant — anchors a lien and a collection claim. The broader playbook is in [the complete guide to contractor disputes](/blog/2026-05-19-complete-guide-contractor-disputes-construction-defects-california).

## Frequently asked questions

**Is a verbal home improvement agreement legal in California?** For work over $500, the law requires a written contract. A verbal deal leaves both sides exposed and can subject the contractor to discipline.

**Can a contractor legally ask for half the money up front?** No. The down payment is capped at the lesser of 10% of the contract price or $1,000. Later payments must follow the work.

**What if my contract is missing required disclosures?** A non-compliant contract can expose the contractor to penalties and weaken their position in a dispute; it may also extend a homeowner's cancellation rights. Keep the contract and raise the gaps in any dispute.

**Do these rules apply to commercial work?** Section 7159 targets **home improvement** (residential) contracts. Commercial construction is governed by different rules, though licensing and lien law still apply.

**Should I have a lawyer review my contract?** For a large project, a short review before signing — or a flat-fee demand letter if a dispute arises — is inexpensive insurance compared with litigating a six-figure remodel gone wrong.

## A homeowner's checklist before signing

Before you sign a home improvement contract in California, confirm each of the following:

- The contractor's **license number** is on the contract, and you've verified it is active and in the right classification on the CSLB website.
- The **total price** is stated in dollars, with a **payment schedule** tied to milestones — not a large lump sum up front.
- The **down payment** is no more than the lesser of 10% or $1,000.
- **Start and completion dates** are included.
- The contract contains the required **notices**, including the Mechanics Lien Warning and your three-day cancellation rights.
- There's a written **change order process** so future extras are documented.
- Proof of **workers' compensation and liability insurance** is provided, and a **bond** where applicable.

If any of these is missing, treat it as a warning sign and ask for a corrected contract before work begins.

## A contractor's checklist to protect payment

The same rules that protect homeowners protect contractors who follow them. To keep your right to get paid solid:

- Keep your **license current** and in the correct classification for every day of the job — § 7031 can otherwise bar your claim and force repayment.
- Use a **written, § 7159-compliant contract** for every residential job over $500.
- Document every change with a **signed change order** stating the added cost before doing the work.
- Serve **preliminary notices** where required to preserve mechanics lien rights (Civil Code § 8200).
- Provide **lien waivers** as you're paid, and keep clean records of invoices, photos, and approvals.

Contractors who treat the paperwork as part of the craft rarely end up fighting for their money; those who cut corners often find the law working against them.

## What happens with senior homeowners and door-to-door contracts?

California adds protections in two situations worth flagging. Contracts signed by **seniors (65 and older)** can carry an enhanced cancellation period, and contracts solicited at the **homeowner's residence** (rather than the contractor's place of business) trigger additional cancellation rights and disclosures. If a contractor failed to give the proper cancellation notice in these situations, the homeowner's right to cancel can be extended well beyond the standard three days. Both homeowners and contractors should know when these heightened rules apply, because getting them wrong can unwind a contract entirely.

In California, the home improvement contract is where the entire relationship is won or lost. Get the written terms, the down payment, the change orders, and the lien waivers right, and most disputes never happen — and the ones that do are far easier to resolve.
