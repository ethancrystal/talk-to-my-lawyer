# How Long Does It Take to Get a Response to a Demand Letter in California?

**Short answer: 10 to 30 days for most California demand letters. Attorney-signed letters on professional letterhead tend to get responses within 10-14 days. Self-written letters average 14-30 days. About 30-40% of demand letters receive no response at all — which is itself actionable information.**

Here's the thing nobody tells you: California has no statute requiring anyone to respond to a demand letter. Zero. The letter isn't a subpoena. It doesn't carry court authority. The recipient can legally ignore it forever without penalty.

So why do people respond? Because the letter makes clear what happens next if they don't. And "what happens next" — a lawsuit, a small claims filing, a regulatory complaint — costs them more than settling now.

## The Response Timeline by Recipient Type

| Recipient | Typical Response Time | Why |
|-----------|----------------------|-----|
| Individual who clearly owes you | 7-14 days | They know the claim is valid. Their only question is whether you'll follow through. |
| Business (small) | 10-21 days | Routes through owner. Decision is personal. |
| Business (corporate) | 21-45 days | Routes through legal department. Bureaucracy adds weeks. |
| Insurance company | 15-40 days (regulated) | Cal. Code Regs. § 2695.5 requires acknowledgment within 15 days |
| Government entity | 30-60+ days | Government Claims Act (Gov. Code § 910-915.4) has its own timeline |
| Judgment-proof individual | Never | Nothing to lose from ignoring it |


## What Affects Response Speed

Three variables predict whether you'll hear back in 10 days or 30:

**The signature.** Attorney-signed letters get responses roughly twice as fast as self-written ones. The recipient reads attorney letterhead and immediately calculates the cost of not responding (a lawsuit they'll need their own attorney to defend). A self-written letter gets filed under "I'll deal with this later."

**The amount.** Smaller claims ($500-$2,000) resolve faster because the cost of fighting them exceeds the cost of paying. When your demand letter is for $1,200 and the recipient knows hiring a lawyer costs $300/hour, the math ends the conversation quickly.

**The deadline you set.** Standard practice in California is 15-30 days. Shorter deadlines (10 days) signal urgency but can backfire — the recipient may feel railroaded and dig in. Longer deadlines (45+ days) signal you're not in a hurry, which reduces pressure.

> **California Cal. Code Regs. § 2695.5(e)** — Every insurer must acknowledge receipt of a communication regarding a claim within 15 calendar days. This is the only context where California law imposes a response timeline to demand-like communications. For all other recipients, response is voluntary.

## What "No Response" Tells You

Silence after your deadline isn't nothing. It's data:

**Day 0-15 silence:** Normal. They're deciding or consulting a lawyer.

**Day 15-30 silence:** They're testing your resolve. Will you actually file?

**Day 30+ silence:** They've decided to ignore it. This tells you they either (a) believe you won't follow through, (b) can't pay, or (c) dispute the claim and are waiting for you to make the next move.

At this point, your options are [filing in small claims court](/blog/2026-06-07-sent-demand-letter-no-response-what-now-california) or sending a final "notice of intent to file" letter. Either way, the demand letter created the evidentiary foundation — you gave them a fair chance to resolve it, and they declined.
