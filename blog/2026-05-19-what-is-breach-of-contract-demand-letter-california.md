# What Is a Breach of Contract Demand Letter and Does It Actually Work?

A breach of contract demand letter is a formal written notice sent to someone who failed to perform their obligations under a contract. It identifies the breach, states the damages, and demands a specific remedy — usually payment or completion of the promised work — within a set deadline. In California, sending a demand letter is often required before certain types of lawsuits and resolves the majority of disputes without litigation.

Think of it as the legal equivalent of clearing your throat before raising your voice. The letter itself isn't a lawsuit, but it signals that one is coming if the other side doesn't act.

## How Does a Breach of Contract Demand Letter Work?

The letter follows a straightforward structure. It identifies the parties, references the contract, describes exactly how the other party breached it, calculates the damages or remedy you're owed, and sets a deadline — typically 15 to 30 days — to resolve the matter.

Under California Civil Code §1492, a party must generally tender performance or demand performance before they can sue for breach. The demand letter satisfies this requirement and creates a paper trail showing you acted in good faith before escalating.

What makes it effective isn't the paper — it's the credibility. A well-drafted letter on attorney letterhead, citing specific contract clauses and California statutes, tells the recipient that you've moved past frustrated phone calls and into legal territory.

## When Should You Send One?

Send a breach of contract demand letter when the other party has clearly failed to deliver what was promised and informal attempts to resolve the issue have gone nowhere. Common scenarios in California include:

A contractor who took your deposit but never completed the work. A business partner who hasn't paid their share. A vendor who delivered defective goods and won't issue a refund. A client who refuses to pay for completed services.

The timing matters. Under California Code of Civil Procedure §337, you have four years to bring a breach of written contract claim, and under §339, two years for oral contracts. But sending the letter early — while the facts are fresh and the other party still has your money — dramatically improves your odds of resolution.

## Does It Actually Get Results?

Yes, and the reason is straightforward: responding to a demand letter is almost always cheaper than responding to a lawsuit. When a contractor or business receives a letter that accurately describes their breach and cites the applicable law, they face a simple calculation. Settle now for a reasonable amount, or pay more later in legal fees, court costs, and potential judgment.

California courts also look favorably on plaintiffs who attempted to resolve disputes before filing suit. Judges notice when one party tried to handle things reasonably and the other stonewalled. That context matters at every stage — from settlement conferences to trial.

## What Should Be Included in the Letter?

A strong breach of contract demand letter includes the date and terms of the original contract, a specific description of what was promised versus what was delivered, the dollar amount of damages or the specific remedy sought, a clear deadline for compliance, and a statement of what legal action will follow if the demand isn't met.

It should cite the relevant California statutes — Civil Code §1550 (elements of a contract), Civil Code §3300 (measure of damages for breach), and any industry-specific regulations that apply. For [contractor disputes](/blog/2026-05-19-contractor-did-shoddy-work-refuses-fix-california), that includes Business & Professions Code §7000 et seq. governing licensed contractors.

What it should not include: emotional language, threats unrelated to legal action, or inflated damage claims. Courts and opposing counsel spot overreach immediately, and it undermines your credibility.
