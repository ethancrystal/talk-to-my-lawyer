# Mechanics Lien vs. Stop Payment Notice vs. Demand Letter vs. Small Claims: How California Contractors Actually Get Paid

**Short answer:** A demand letter is the fast, cheap first move. A stop payment notice intercepts undisbursed project funds. A mechanics lien clouds the property's title. Small claims court is the lawyer-free backstop for amounts up to $12,500. Most California contractors get paid fastest by starting with a demand letter and escalating only as deadlines and leverage require.

When a client or general contractor won't pay, contractors often reach for whatever tool they've heard of — and pick the wrong one for the situation. California actually offers four distinct remedies, each aimed at a different pressure point. Understanding what each one does is the difference between getting paid in two weeks and chasing money for a year.

## Tool 1: The demand letter — speed and low cost

A demand letter is a written notice stating what you're owed, referencing your contract, and giving a deadline to pay before you escalate.

**What it does:** Applies immediate, documented pressure. It tells the other side you're organized, you know your rights, and you're ready to act.

**Cost and speed:** The cheapest tool, and it can go out the same day. A flat-fee attorney letter costs a fraction of a lien or lawsuit.

**Leverage:** Moderate. It works best when the relationship isn't fully broken or when the other side simply needs a push. It also preserves goodwill better than a recorded lien.

**Deadlines:** None to send — but don't let the demand-letter stage burn your **mechanics lien deadline** in the background.

A demand letter doubles as a [Notice of Intent to Lien](/blog/2026-06-02-what-is-a-notice-of-intent-to-lien-california) when it warns that a lien is coming next.

## Tool 2: The stop payment notice — reach the money directly

A **stop payment notice** (Civil Code § 8500 and following) is served on the owner or the construction lender, directing them to withhold undisbursed funds to cover your claim.

**What it does:** Freezes money still sitting in the project before it flows down (or back) to someone who won't pay you. On lender-financed jobs, a bonded stop notice can reach construction loan funds.

**Cost and speed:** Moderate. There are formalities and timing rules, but it can be powerful when there's undisbursed money to capture.

**Leverage:** High — when funds remain. It's useless if the project is fully paid out, which is why timing matters.

**Best for:** Subcontractors and suppliers on financed projects where the owner or lender still holds money.

## Tool 3: The mechanics lien — cloud the title

A mechanics lien attaches to the **property itself**, clouding title until resolved.

**What it does:** Blocks or complicates a sale or refinance, which gives owners a strong reason to pay or settle.

**Cost and speed:** Higher. Recording has fees, and enforcing the lien generally means a foreclosure lawsuit.

**Deadlines (strict):**

- Serve a **20-day preliminary notice** to preserve rights as a sub/supplier (Civil Code § 8200).
- Record within about **90 days of completion** (direct contractor), or **60 days after a recorded Notice of Completion** (Civil Code § 8412–8414).
- File a **foreclosure action within 90 days of recording** or the lien lapses (Civil Code § 8460).

**Leverage:** The highest of the four — but only if you preserved your rights and hit every deadline.

## Tool 4: Small claims court — the lawyer-free backstop

Small claims is California's fast, informal court for smaller disputes.

**What it does:** Gets you a judgment without lawyers or complex procedure.

**Cost and speed:** Low filing cost; hearings are scheduled within weeks to a few months. No attorneys represent parties at the hearing.

**Limit:** **$12,500 for an individual** (business entities are capped lower). For bigger claims, you'd use civil court instead.

**Leverage:** A judgment you can enforce — but collection still requires effort, and the limit rules out large project balances.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Tool | Speed | Cost | Leverage | Key deadline | Best when |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Demand letter** | Same day | Lowest | Moderate | None to send | First move; relationship intact |
| **Stop payment notice** | Days | Moderate | High (if funds remain) | Timing rules apply | Money still in the project |
| **Mechanics lien** | Requires recording | Higher | Highest | 90 days to record; 90 to foreclose | Owner has equity; large balance |
| **Small claims** | Weeks–months | Low | Judgment | 4-yr SOL (CCP § 337) | Claim ≤ $12,500; good docs |

## How should a contractor sequence these?

1. **Preserve rights first.** Serve the preliminary notice at the start of every job so the lien and stop-notice options stay open.
2. **Lead with the demand letter.** It's cheapest and resolves most disputes. Make it a Notice of Intent to Lien so the threat is explicit.
3. **Add a stop payment notice** if there's undisbursed money to reach — it can capture funds before they disappear.
4. **Record the mechanics lien** within your deadline if payment still hasn't come. This is your heaviest leverage.
5. **File in small claims** (≤ $12,500) or civil court (larger amounts, or to foreclose the lien) as the backstop.

## Don't forget interest and penalties

All paths can recover more than the base debt. California allows **10% annual prejudgment interest** on a liquidated contract debt (Civil Code § 3289), and prompt-payment statutes — such as Business & Professions Code § 7108.5's **2%-per-month** penalty for wrongfully withheld subcontractor payments — raise the cost of stalling. Spell these out in your demand so doing nothing becomes expensive for the other side.

## Which tool fits your role in the project?

Your position in the contracting chain narrows the choice:

- **Direct (prime) contractor dealing with the owner.** You have the cleanest path to a mechanics lien and a straightforward breach of contract claim. A demand letter plus a recorded lien (within deadline) is usually your strongest combination.
- **Subcontractor or supplier.** Your lien and stop-notice rights often depend on having served a **20-day preliminary notice** (Civil Code § 8200). If you did, you have the full toolkit. If you didn't, you may be limited to a demand letter and a contract claim against whoever hired you.
- **Working on a lender-financed project.** A **stop payment notice** can reach undisbursed loan funds — sometimes more practical than a lien when the owner has little equity.
- **Owed a smaller, well-documented balance.** Small claims may be the most efficient route, skipping the cost of lien enforcement entirely.

Matching the tool to your role prevents wasted effort — for example, recording a lien when you never preserved the right, or filing in small claims for an amount above the $12,500 cap.

## A worked example

Say you're a remodeling subcontractor owed $9,000 on a financed home renovation, and you served your preliminary notice at the start. The efficient sequence looks like this: send a **demand letter that doubles as a Notice of Intent to Lien** with a 10-day deadline; if the lender still holds funds, serve a **stop payment notice** to freeze enough to cover your claim; if the deadline passes, **record your mechanics lien** before the 90-day window closes; and if it still isn't resolved, file in **small claims** since $9,000 is under the $12,500 individual limit. At each rung you give the other side a clean chance to pay before you spend more — and you never let a deadline lapse in the background.

## The bottom line

These four tools aren't competitors — they're a ladder. The contractors who get paid fastest preserve their lien rights early, open with a sharp demand letter, and climb only as high as each situation requires. ## Frequently asked questions

**Can I send a demand letter and record a lien at the same time?** Yes. In fact, a demand letter framed as a Notice of Intent to Lien warns of the lien before you record it, which often produces payment without recording anything — while keeping the lien ready if the deadline approaches.

**What if I missed the preliminary notice deadline?** You may lose mechanics lien and stop-notice rights for work outside the 20-day window, but you can still pursue a demand letter and a breach of contract claim against whoever hired you. Talk to counsel about your specific dates.

**Is small claims worth it for a construction debt?** For documented claims up to $12,500 (individual), yes — it's fast and lawyer-free. For larger balances or to foreclose a lien, you'll need civil court instead.

**Do these tools work against a homeowner who already paid the general contractor?** A subcontractor's lien can still attach to the property even if the owner paid the GC, which is why owners should collect lien waivers. As the unpaid sub, your lien and stop-notice rights run against the project, not just the GC's wallet.
