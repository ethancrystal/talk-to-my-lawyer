**Short answer:** A 3-day notice starts the eviction clock. A demand letter tries to get you paid before you need that clock. Eviction gets you possession and a judgment when nothing else worked. They're a sequence, not alternatives — and most California landlords use only one of the three.

---

When a tenant stops paying rent, California landlords have three distinct legal tools. Each one does a different job. Confusing them — or skipping the cheaper ones — is how a $3,000 rent dispute becomes a $7,000 legal problem.

Here's the full breakdown.

---

## Tool 1: The Attorney Demand Letter

### What it is

A formal written demand for payment, signed by a licensed California attorney. Not a form from the internet. Not a letter you wrote in a frustrated moment at 11pm. A document with legal letterhead stating: you owe this amount, here's the legal basis, pay within 10-14 days, or legal proceedings follow.

### What it does

It converts a landlord-tenant dispute into something that looks, to the tenant, like a legal case already in motion. The letter signals that an attorney has reviewed the matter, the amount owed has been calculated and documented, and the next step isn't another angry text — it's a filing.

For tenants who care about their rental history, their credit record, or avoiding a public court judgment: this is often enough. A judgment in an unlawful detainer case is a matter of public record. It appears on tenant screening reports. Many tenants, understanding this, would rather pay than be judged.

### What it costs

**First letter: $0 at TTML.** Subsequent letters and services are flat-fee.

### Timeline

Draft to delivery: 2-5 days. Payment window: 10-14 days. Total: **2-3 weeks** to know if it's working.

### When it works

- Tenant has the ability to pay but is avoiding the conversation
- Tenant cares about their rental history or credit record
- Dispute involves a clear, calculable amount the tenant acknowledges owing
- This is the first serious legal step you've taken

### When it doesn't work

- Tenant has already vacated with nothing
- Tenant has no assets and knows it
- Tenant is experienced at gaming the eviction timeline and is deliberately waiting

## Tool 2: The 3-Day Notice to Pay or Quit

### What it is

A statutory legal notice required by California Code of Civil Procedure § 1161(2) as a prerequisite for filing an unlawful detainer (eviction) complaint. Without a properly served 3-day notice, the court will dismiss your eviction case.

### What it does

It creates the legal foundation for eviction. That's its job. It tells the tenant: pay the rent owed, or vacate the premises, within 3 calendar days. If neither happens, the landlord can file for unlawful detainer.

What it doesn't do is threaten anything beyond that. Many tenants know the eviction timeline: 3-day notice, then filing, then 5-day response period, then a hearing date that's often 20+ days away. A tenant deliberately playing the clock reads a 3-day notice as the start of a 3-month process, not an imminent threat.

### What it costs

**$0 to prepare and serve yourself.** If you hire an attorney to draft and serve it: $150-$400.

### Timeline

Preparation: 1 day. Service: 1 day. Waiting period: 3 calendar days. Total: **about 5 days** before you can file.

### Service requirements

California law requires specific service methods for the 3-day notice:

1. **Personal delivery** to the tenant (preferred, easiest to prove)
2. **Substituted service** — leaving a copy with a person of suitable age at the premises AND mailing a copy to the tenant (the mailing extends the notice period by 5 additional days)
3. **Nail and mail** — posting on front door AND mailing (same 5-day extension applies)

Keep a proof of service document. Write down the date, time, method, and the name of whoever received the notice.

### One important trap

Accepting rent — even partial rent — after serving a 3-day notice may "cure" the notice and give the tenant the right to stay. If a tenant hands you money after you've served the notice, consult an attorney before you accept it.

### When to use it

- Before filing an eviction (mandatory)
- As a signal that legal proceedings are imminent (simultaneously with the demand letter, or after the demand letter period ends)

## Tool 3: Unlawful Detainer (Eviction)

### What it is

A summary court proceeding that gives a California landlord the right to recover possession of real property from a tenant who has violated the lease — most commonly by not paying rent.

### What it does

A successful unlawful detainer case gives you two things: (1) a judgment for possession of the unit, enforced by the sheriff, and (2) a money judgment for the back rent owed, plus potentially court costs and attorney fees if your lease has a prevailing-party clause (Civil Code § 1717).

What a judgment does *not* do: put money in your pocket automatically. A judgment is a legal right to collect. If your tenant has no wages, no bank account, no assets you can reach, collecting that judgment is a separate project that can take years.

### What it costs

**Filing fees:** $200-$500 depending on the court and amount claimed.

**Attorney fees:** $1,500-$4,000 for an uncontested case handled by a California attorney. $3,000-$7,000+ if the tenant contests.

**Your time:** Even with an attorney, you'll attend a hearing. Plan for at minimum 1-2 court appearances.

**Indirect costs:** 1-3 months of ongoing nonpayment while the case proceeds. Potential vacancy after judgment while you find a new tenant.

### Timeline

| Step | Time |
|------|------|
| Serve 3-day notice | Day 0 |
| Wait out 3 days | Days 1-3 |
| File unlawful detainer complaint | Day 4+ |
| Tenant served | Days 5-10 |
| Tenant response period | 5 calendar days |
| Hearing date (if uncontested) | 10-20 days after service |
| Judgment | At hearing |
| Writ of possession issued | 1-3 days post-judgment |
| Sheriff lockout | 5-10 days after writ |
| **Total (best case)** | **45-60 days** |
| **Total (contested/backlogged court)** | **90-180 days** |

### When to use it

- The demand letter and 3-day notice have both produced nothing
- You need the tenant out, regardless of whether you'll ever collect the money
- The amount owed is large enough to justify the cost of the proceeding
- You have documentation (lease, rent ledger, proof of service on the 3-day notice)

## The Right Sequence

Most landlords should run this in order:

1. **Attorney demand letter** (free, 2-3 weeks): Does this resolve voluntarily?
2. **3-day notice** (required before eviction): Served simultaneously or after the letter fails.
3. **Unlawful detainer** (expensive, slow): Only after both prior steps have produced nothing.

Skipping 1 and 2 and going straight to 3 is the most common expensive mistake California landlords make. The letter costs nothing and takes 2 weeks. The eviction costs $2,000-$7,000 and takes 2-6 months. If there's a reasonable chance the letter works, doing it first is obvious.
