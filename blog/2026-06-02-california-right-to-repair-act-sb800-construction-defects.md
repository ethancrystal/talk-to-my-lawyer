# California's Right to Repair Act (SB 800): How Homeowners Must Handle Construction Defects Before Suing

**Short answer:** California's Right to Repair Act (Civil Code § 895 and following, often called SB 800) governs construction defect claims for new homes sold on or after January 1, 2003. It sets building standards and requires homeowners to give the builder written notice and an opportunity to repair before filing most lawsuits. Missing the prelawsuit process can stall or derail your case.

Discovering that your newer home has a serious defect — water intrusion, cracked foundations, failing plumbing — is stressful enough. What surprises many California homeowners is that they can't simply sue the builder the next day. For homes sold after 2003, the Right to Repair Act lays out a specific, mandatory pathway. Understanding it protects both your rights and your timeline.

## What is the Right to Repair Act and who does it cover?

The Right to Repair Act (Civil Code § 895–945.5) was enacted to standardize how residential construction defect claims work. It applies to **new residential units sold on or after January 1, 2003** — primarily homes purchased from a builder or developer. It sets out detailed **building standards** (called "functionality standards") covering water, structural, soil, plumbing, electrical, and other systems. If a component falls short of those standards, it can be a defect you can pursue — without always having to prove the defect caused other damage first.

Older homes, and many homes bought used rather than from the builder, may fall outside SB 800 and instead proceed under ordinary contract and tort law. That distinction matters, so it's worth confirming early.

## What building standards does SB 800 set?

The Act lists specific performance standards a home must meet. In broad terms, they address things like:

- **Water issues** — roofs, windows, doors, decks, and walls that shouldn't allow unintended water intrusion.
- **Structural issues** — foundations, framing, and load-bearing components.
- **Soil issues** — proper drainage and soil conditions around the structure.
- **Plumbing, electrical, and mechanical systems** — that should function as intended for defined periods.

Because the standards are concrete, a homeowner often doesn't have to wait for catastrophic damage; a component that violates a functionality standard can itself be actionable.

## What is the prelawsuit "notice and repair" process?

This is the core of SB 800 and what trips up unprepared homeowners. Before filing most construction defect lawsuits covered by the Act, you generally must:

1. **Give the builder written notice** of the claimed defect, describing it in reasonable detail.
2. **Allow the builder to inspect and test** the alleged defect within the statutory timeframes.
3. **Give the builder the opportunity to offer a repair** (or, in some cases, a cash settlement) before litigation proceeds.

The builder, in turn, must respond and act within defined deadlines. If the builder fails to follow the process — for example, by not responding or not making an adequate offer — the homeowner may be released to proceed to litigation. This back-and-forth is mandatory for covered claims, which is why your **written notice** is such an important document: it starts the clock and frames the dispute.

## Where does a demand letter fit?

A well-drafted notice letter is effectively your entry point into the SB 800 process. It should identify the home, describe each defect against the relevant functionality standard, and formally invoke the builder's right (and obligation) to inspect and repair. Done correctly, it both satisfies the prelawsuit requirement and puts the builder on notice that you understand the statute. A poorly drafted or premature lawsuit, by contrast, can be stayed until the process is completed. The structure of a [breach of contract demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) is a useful starting point, tailored to the Act's notice requirements.

## What are the deadlines for construction defect claims?

Two timing layers matter:

- **SB 800's own limitation periods.** The Act sets time limits tied to specific building components — some systems carry shorter windows (e.g., a few years for certain finishes) and others longer.
- **California's general construction statutes of repose.** For latent (hidden) defects, Code of Civil Procedure § 337.15 sets an outer limit of **10 years** from substantial completion. For patent (obvious) defects, Code of Civil Procedure § 337.1 sets a **4-year** limit. These act as backstops beyond which claims are generally barred.

Because the periods vary by defect type and overlap in complex ways, homeowners should calendar deadlines conservatively and seek advice well before any limit approaches. Waiting too long can extinguish an otherwise valid claim.

## What can you recover under the Right to Repair Act?

Depending on the facts, recovery can include the **cost of repairing the defect**, the cost of repairing damage the defect caused, the reasonable cost of temporary housing during repairs, and certain other related costs the Act enumerates. The goal is to make the homeowner whole for the violation of the building standards — not to provide a windfall. An attorney can help you understand which categories realistically apply to your situation.

## Should you use the builder's warranty instead?

Many builders provide an express warranty, and SB 800 allows builders to offer their own enhanced warranty and prelitigation procedures in some circumstances. You may have overlapping options — the statutory process, an express warranty, and ordinary contract remedies. The best path depends on what the builder offered, what the defect is, and how much time has passed. Don't assume the builder's warranty is your only route, and don't sign away rights without understanding them.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I have to let the builder repair the defect?** For covered SB 800 claims, you generally must give the builder notice and an opportunity to inspect and repair before suing. Skipping that step can get your lawsuit stayed.

**My home is from 2010 — am I covered?** Likely yes, if you bought a new residential unit sold on or after January 1, 2003. Confirm the sale date and whether you were the original purchaser, as those facts affect coverage.

**How long do I have to bring a construction defect claim?** It depends on the defect. Latent defects carry an outer limit of 10 years (CCP § 337.15); patent defects, 4 years (CCP § 337.1); and SB 800 sets component-specific periods. Calendar deadlines early.

**Is the Right to Repair Act my only remedy?** Not necessarily. Express warranties and ordinary contract claims may also apply. An attorney can map every available path before you commit to one.

**Do I need a lawyer for an SB 800 claim?** Construction defect litigation is technical, but the first step — a proper notice/demand letter — is something many homeowners handle with attorney help at a predictable cost, often resolving the dispute before full litigation.

## How does the process actually unfold, step by step?

It helps to see the sequence as a timeline rather than a single event:

1. **Document the defect.** Photograph the problem, keep maintenance records, and note when you first noticed it. Dated evidence matters because the Act's timelines run from defined points, and a builder will scrutinize whether the issue is a defect or ordinary wear.
2. **Serve written notice on the builder.** Describe each claimed defect in reasonable detail and reference the relevant functionality standard. This is the document that opens the statutory process.
3. **Allow inspection and testing.** The builder is entitled to inspect, and sometimes to test, within the statutory windows. Cooperating protects your claim; stonewalling can be held against you.
4. **Receive the builder's offer (or non-response).** The builder may offer to repair, offer cash, or do nothing. Each path has consequences: an adequate, timely repair may resolve the claim; an inadequate offer or silence can release you to litigate.
5. **Evaluate the repair.** If the builder repairs, assess whether the work actually corrected the defect. A failed or cosmetic repair can reopen your options.
6. **Proceed to litigation or settlement if unresolved.** Only after the prelawsuit process — or the builder's failure to follow it — does a lawsuit typically move forward.

Treating each step as a documented, deadline-aware milestone keeps your strongest remedies alive and prevents the builder from later arguing you skipped a required step.

## What mistakes do homeowners make under SB 800?

The most common errors are avoidable. Some homeowners **wait too long**, letting a limitation period lapse before sending notice. Others **sue first and notify later**, only to have the case stayed until they complete the mandatory prelawsuit process. Some **accept a quick cosmetic repair** that hides rather than fixes the defect, then discover the problem returns after their options have narrowed. And many **fail to document** the defect's history, leaving them unable to show when it appeared or how it violates a building standard. Each of these mistakes can be prevented by approaching the claim methodically and getting advice early, while every deadline is still open.

## How does SB 800 interact with a mechanics lien or contract claim?

SB 800 governs the **defect** relationship between a homeowner and the builder of a newer home. It runs alongside — not in place of — other construction-law tools. If a separate contractor performed later work and wasn't paid, mechanics lien law (Civil Code § 8400 and following) still applies to that dispute. If your claim is really about an unpaid balance or a breach of your construction contract rather than a building-standard defect, an ordinary [breach of contract demand letter](/blog/2026-05-19-what-is-breach-of-contract-demand-letter-california) and the four-year contract limitation (Code of Civil Procedure § 337) may be the better fit. Identifying which body of law your problem falls under is the first strategic decision, and it determines every deadline that follows.

The Right to Repair Act is designed to give builders a chance to fix problems and give homeowners a clear path to recovery when they don't. Use the process deliberately: document the defects, send proper notice, respect the deadlines, and keep your strongest remedies open.
