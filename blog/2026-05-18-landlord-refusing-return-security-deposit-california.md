# My Landlord Is Refusing to Return My Security Deposit — What Can I Do in California?

California law gives your landlord exactly 21 days to return your security deposit after you move out. If they miss that deadline — or make deductions they can't legally justify — you have real options, including a bad-faith penalty worth twice the amount wrongfully withheld.

## What Does California Law Actually Say About Security Deposits?

California Civil Code § 1950.5 is one of the most tenant-friendly security deposit statutes in the country. It does several things at once: it caps how much a landlord can charge (two months' rent for an unfurnished unit, three months' for furnished), it defines exactly what a deposit can be used for, and it sets strict rules about when and how the money must be returned.

The statute isn't advisory. It's mandatory. A landlord who ignores it faces real financial consequences — not just the obligation to return what's owed, but potentially a penalty on top.

## The 21-Day Rule: Your Landlord's Most Important Deadline

Under Civil Code § 1950.5(g), your landlord has 21 calendar days after you vacate the unit to do one of two things: (1) return your entire deposit, or (2) send you an itemized written statement explaining every deduction, along with copies of receipts or invoices for any work done, plus whatever portion of the deposit they aren't keeping.


Twenty-one days sounds reasonable until you realize that many landlords either don't know the rule or are counting on the fact that most tenants won't follow up. If the 21-day window closes and you've received nothing — no check, no statement, no itemized list — the landlord is in violation of the statute. That matters.

If they send you a statement but the deductions are vague, unsupported, or for things that aren't allowed under California law, you can challenge those too.

## What Deductions Are Actually Legal?

Civil Code § 1950.5(b) limits what a landlord can take from a security deposit to four things: unpaid rent, cleaning the unit if it wasn't returned in the same condition it was rented (minus normal wear and tear), repairing damage you caused beyond normal wear and tear, and costs associated with restoring or replacing personal property you agreed to return.

That last category matters less than the first three. The key phrase you'll see in every security deposit dispute is "normal wear and tear" — and California courts have been pretty clear about what that means. Scuffs on walls from furniture, minor carpet wear, small nail holes from hanging pictures: all normal. Stained carpet from a pet accident, large holes in drywall, broken fixtures: not normal.

A landlord who charges you $800 to repaint an apartment you lived in for three years is almost certainly making an illegal deduction. Paint fades. It has a useful life. You aren't obligated to leave the unit looking like it did the day you moved in.

## What Happens When a Landlord Violates the 21-Day Rule?

This is where Civil Code § 1950.5(l) comes in. If a court finds that the landlord acted in bad faith — either by failing to return the deposit on time or by making deductions they knew weren't justified — the court can award you the actual amount wrongfully withheld plus a penalty of up to twice that amount.


So if your landlord wrongfully kept $2,000 of your deposit, a court could order them to pay you up to $6,000: the $2,000 back plus a $4,000 bad-faith penalty. That's not a guarantee — courts look at the totality of the circumstances — but it's a real possibility that a landlord who simply ignores the statute or makes up deductions has to reckon with.

## What's Your First Move?

Before you file anything, send a written demand. This accomplishes two things: it gives the landlord a formal, documented opportunity to do the right thing (which a meaningful percentage will take when they realize you know the law), and it establishes your position in writing before any legal proceeding.

The demand should cite the statute, state the amount you're owed, set a response deadline — typically 14 days — and make clear what your next step will be if they don't respond.

A demand letter signed by an attorney carries more weight than one you write yourself. Landlords who shrug off a tenant's email tend to respond differently when they receive a letter on law firm letterhead that cites specific code sections and outlines potential liability. For more on what makes a demand letter effective, see [what a demand letter actually does](/blog/2026-05-14-what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices-does-it-work).

If the demand doesn't resolve it, small claims court is your next option. California small claims handles disputes up to $12,500 for individuals (CCP § 116.220), which covers most security deposit cases. Filing costs around $30–$75, you don't need a lawyer in the room, and you can typically get a hearing date within 30–70 days.

## The Documentation That Wins Cases

Whatever path you take, your evidence determines the outcome. Before you move out — and immediately after — take timestamped photographs of every room, every wall, every appliance, and every fixture. Walk through the unit with a camera and narrate what you're seeing. If you did a move-in inspection, find those forms.

If you cleaned the unit professionally, keep the receipt. If you had the carpets cleaned, keep the receipt. The goal is to make it as hard as possible for a landlord to claim you left the place in worse shape than you found it.

## What to Do Right Now

If you're past the 21-day mark and haven't received your deposit or an itemized statement, the landlord has already violated Civil Code § 1950.5. Your first step is to send a written demand documenting the violation and demanding return of the full deposit.
